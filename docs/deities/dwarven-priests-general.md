# Dwarven Priests -- General & Subrace Notes

Shared flavor for the Dwarven Pantheon (the Morndinsamman) that applies across the individual deity
entries, drawn from the Dwarven Pantheon's introductory prose and Appendix 1's "Dwarven Priests" and
"Gray Dwarves (Duergar)" discussion. Mechanical crunch specific to 2e classes (level caps, multiclass
legality tables, the exact 40%-wild-magic percentage, and similar) has been dropped; the flavor and
setting context that still matters for play has been kept.

## The Morndinsamman

The Stout Folk of the Realms worship a pantheon collectively called the Morndinsamman, loosely
translated as "shield brothers on high" or "the high dwarves." Its exact composition varies from clan
to clan, but formal membership is determined by Moradin alone. The good and neutral dwarven gods --
Moradin, Berronar, Clangeddin, Dugmaren, Dumathoin, Gorm, Haela, Marthammor, Sharindlar, Thard Harr,
and Vergadain -- have always been members in good standing. Abbathor remains a member despite his
treachery never having been definitively proven, though most of his fellows detest him. Laduguer was
banished by the All-Father long ago, and Deep Duerra was exiled immediately upon her apotheosis; both
are considered members-in-exile rather than true outsiders. Diirinka and his mad twin Diinkarazan are
the only dwarven powers no longer considered part of the Morndinsamman at all -- they and their
derro followers have largely been forgotten by the Stout Folk, remembered only by a handful of sages.

## Origins and Cosmology

The dwarven gods are said to have sprung from stone and earth, beginning with Moradin. Berronar is
universally held to be his wife, and most dwarven theologians consider the rest of the pantheon their
descendants, though the exact ancestry varies by myth. After Moradin and Berronar, the oldest dwarven
powers are thought to be Dumathoin, Abbathor, Laduguer, Clangeddin, Sharindlar, and the twins
Diinkarazan and Diirinka. The next generation includes Thard Harr, Gorm Gulthyn, Marthammor Duin, and
Dugmaren Brightmantle. Haela Brightaxe and Deep Duerra, the most recent additions, are said to be
Moradin and Berronar's grandchildren.

The dwarven pantheon is predominantly male, reflecting the population imbalance between the sexes
among mortal dwarves. Unlike the elven gods, the Morndinsamman are scattered across the Outer Planes
rather than dwelling together -- dwarven theologians read this as symbolic of the dwarven hunger for
territory and living space, mirroring how mortal dwarves are ever pushing into new ground below the
surface. An oddity of the dwarven deities is that most can, if they wish, manifest their avatars at
huge stature (up to 20 feet tall, in Moradin's case), reflecting their activist natures and roles as
inspirational leaders of their people.

Moradin is said to have secretly fashioned the first dwarves from iron and mithral in his Soul Forge,
beating their bodies into shape with his great hammer and breathing on his creations to cool them and
grant them souls. Dwarven smiths have long drawn a connection between procreation and metalcraft --
more than one has looked on a finished piece of work and felt as though they had breathed life and a
soul of sorts into the metal, as Moradin once did. Moradin himself taught the first dwarves smithing,
metalworking, toolmaking, and weaponcrafting, and still watches over those crafts today. No dwarven
deity has a sacred or totem animal, and none of their holy symbols depict a living creature -- a
consequence of Moradin's ruling that dwarves must hold no other race above them, since an animal
symbol would imply that creature's superiority. Likewise, no dwarf or part of one is ever used as a
holy symbol, since Moradin taught that dwarves must never worship each other.

Many founding myths have the earliest dwarves fighting their way up from the world's core to the
mountains above, overcoming great monsters and physical hazards along the way through strength,
combat, and physical skill rather than wit or trickery. This pragmatism runs through all of dwarven
theology: there is no place for the arcane or the mystical in dwarven myth, legend, or belief.

## Racial History and the Subraces

Most dwarven legends trace the earliest Realms settlements of the Stout Folk to the Yehimal, tens of
thousands of years ago, before a great exodus split the race into branches that spread across Faerûn,
Kara-Tur, and Zakhara. Those who reached Faerûn settled first beneath modern-day Semphar. The first
great dwarven kingdom in Faerûn rose in the cavern of Bhaerynden beneath the Shaar; the first major
schism came with the founding of Shanatar beneath Amn, Erlkazar, Tethyr, Calimshan, the Lands of the
Lion, and the Lake of Steam, giving rise to the shield dwarves (mountain dwarves), who went on to
found most of the great dwarven nations of the North. From the earliest shield dwarves, Dumathoin
created the urdunnirin. When the Crown Wars and the descent of the drow drove southern Faerûn's
dwarves into exile, those who fled as far as the jungles of Chult interbred with dwarves already
living there, becoming the ancestors of the wild dwarves (jungle dwarves). When the cavern of
Bhaerynden later collapsed to form the Great Rift, the dwarves who resettled around it became the gold
dwarves (hill dwarves). The last great schism came when an entire clan of shield dwarves, Clan
Duergar, was enslaved by illithids; the survivors, later known as the gray dwarves (duergar), spread
through much of the Underdark during their long absence from the rest of dwarven civilization. Other
minor branches -- the desert dwarves of Maztica, the arctic dwarves of the Great Glacier, and the
albino dwarves of Chult -- are simply isolated shield dwarf clans. (Legends of aquatic dwarves in the
Sea of Fallen Stars have been thoroughly discredited.)

These racial schisms are mirrored in worship. Shield dwarves and gold dwarves still perceive and
revere the Morndinsamman similarly, broadly acknowledging all of the High Dwarves. Gray dwarves,
however, venerate Laduguer and Deep Duerra almost to the exclusion of the other dwarven gods; the
legendary derro speak only of Diirinka (and occasionally Diinkarazan); and the wild dwarves revere
only Thard Harr.

## Decline, Doubt, and New Cults

Some dwarves blame the gods for the race's slow decline across Faerûn, or feel the old powers have
grown too weak or too out of touch to guide the Stout Folk through the ages to come. Many dwarves have
dabbled in new beliefs as a result -- movements urging mastery of wizardry as the key to survival, or
quiet interbreeding and diplomacy with humans and gnomes meant to eventually absorb those more fecund
races -- most of which come and go as passing fads embraced briefly by each new generation. A few such
cults have proven durable enough that some divine power (a dwarven deity working in secret, a power of
another race angling for influence, or even an unknown dwarven god) has come to support their pleas by
granting spells to their priests -- Abbathor's Wyrm Cult and Clangeddin's Axe Dwarves, described in
their own entries, are the best-documented examples.

## High Old Ones

Dwarven priests of 7th level or higher are collectively known as High Old Ones, though each individual
faith has its own title for the rank. They are the most respected elders of the Stout Folk, especially
in the North, where clan power and the pride and prosperity of young dwarves runs weakest, and they
often act as direct servants and speakers for their deities. Dwarves of every race and faith --
including gray dwarves, wild dwarves, and even the followers of evil powers such as Abbathor, Laduguer,
and Deep Duerra -- respect High Old Ones and will not knowingly attack one, whatever the circumstances.
High Old Ones are also the only dwarven priests capable of working dwarven rune magic.

## Secrecy from Nondwarves

Dwarven priests try to hide their calling from nondwarves, casting spells from hiding or at a distance
whenever they can, dressing and behaving like any other dwarf, and keeping worship and ritual hidden
from outside eyes. They have largely succeeded in keeping even the existence of their priesthoods
secret from most nondwarves in Faerûn, particularly in the North, where dwarves live more closely
among other peoples and tread more carefully as a result. There is a subtle look about the eyes and
face of a dwarven priest, readily recognized by another dwarf of the same race in good light and
within twenty feet, but never by strangers or nondwarves -- a quiet mark of devotion, not an obvious
badge of rank or faith.

## The Stone or Metal Holy Symbol

Dwarven priests channel their spells through a stone or metal holy symbol worn against the skin or
held in hand while casting. Without that stabilizing focus, or if the symbol is lost, stolen, or
destroyed mid-casting, a dwarven priest's spells become dangerously unstable and unpredictable --
which is also why most dwarven priests carry spare holy symbols at all times. This same instability is
said to be why dwarven priest spells lean so heavily on material components: physical anchors from the
Prime, not subject to any innate magical resistance. Granted powers that aren't spells (as opposed to
spells themselves) are never affected by this instability. Thard Harr's priests are the one known
exception to the stone-or-metal rule: the Disentangler's faithful must instead keep physical contact
with bare earth or a plant rooted in it to cast safely.

## Multiclassing and Gender

Most dwarven priests who multiclass do so as fighter/clerics, though a handful of faiths permit other
combinations -- Abbathor and Vergadain allow cleric/thieves, and Clangeddin Silverbeard and Haela
Brightaxe allow fighter/specialty priests. Before the Time of Troubles, a dwarven deity's priesthood
was almost always restricted to worshipers of that deity's own gender; since the Godswar this is no
longer an absolute rule, and every dwarven faith now accepts priests of either gender, if reluctantly.
Priests of the gender opposite their deity's are still likely to be treated gingerly, or with a degree
of resentment, by their same-gender peers, and are more often called upon to prove their commitment.
Dwarven culture is very slow to adopt new customs.

## Gray Dwarves (Duergar)

Gray dwarven priests are dwarven priests in every sense described above, with the racial abilities and
weaknesses of the duergar layered on top: an innate capacity for enlargement and invisibility, bonuses
to avoid surprise, susceptibility to bright light, and immunity to poison, paralysis, and
illusion/phantasm effects. Many duergar also carry at least some latent psionic ability -- most gray
dwarven priests amount to priest/psionicists in practice, and even those without formal training are
typically wild talents. This reflects the duergar's deep cultural and religious ties to Deep Duerra
and the Invisible Art, and the gray dwarves' broader estrangement from the shield, gold, and wild
dwarven kin who still hold to the wider Morndinsamman.
