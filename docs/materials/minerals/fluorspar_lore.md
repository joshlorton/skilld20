# Fluorspar (Fluorite) -- Lore Research Entry

## Mineralogical Identity

**Name:** Fluorite (from Latin *fluere*, "to flow," originally applied because the mineral was used as a flux in metal smelting -- lowering the melting point of ores and causing the slag to flow more easily); Fluorspar (the older and still-current English mining/trade term, essentially synonymous with fluorite, "spar" being a traditional English term for any easily-cleaved, glassy-lustered non-metallic mineral)
**Alt names:** Murrina / Murrhina / Myrrhina (the Latin term used by Pliny the Elder and other Roman writers, identified by the leading modern scholarly consensus -- though not unanimously -- with fluorite; see Source Quality Summary below for the genuinely contested nature of this identification); Blue John / Derbyshire Blue John (the specific banded purple-blue-yellow English variety found only at Castleton, Derbyshire); Fluorspar (used interchangeably with fluorite in mining and industrial contexts, especially in older literature)
**Entry type:** Type 5 -- Disputed Material. This is the first entry in this project where the central historical question -- whether a specific, extensively documented and extremely valuable ancient luxury good (Roman murrhine ware) is actually made of this mineral at all -- remains an active and unresolved scholarly debate spanning more than two centuries (1781 to the present), rather than a settled identification with disputed peripheral details.
**Hardness:** 4 Mohs (notably soft for an ornamental stone -- this is the reference mineral for hardness 4 on the Mohs scale itself, one of only ten minerals chosen by Friedrich Mohs in 1812 to define his original hardness scale)
**Crystal system:** Isometric (cubic); calcium fluoride, chemical formula CaF₂
**Color range:** Among the most chromatically variable minerals in the world -- colorless and pure in its theoretical ideal state, but trace impurities and structural defects routinely produce purple, blue, green, yellow, pink, red, brown, black, or colorless material, frequently banded or zoned with multiple colors within a single specimen (the banding being the visually defining feature of the Derbyshire Blue John variety specifically)
**Notable property -- fluorescence:** Many fluorite specimens glow visibly under ultraviolet light, a phenomenon so closely associated with this mineral that the general physical phenomenon itself was named after it. This naming event is precisely documented: the Irish physicist and mathematician George Gabriel Stokes coined the term "fluorescence" in 1852, explicitly deriving it from "fluorite," after studying the mineral's light-emission properties (Wikipedia, corroborated as standard physics-history fact). This is one of the cleanest cases in this entire project of a scientific vocabulary word being coined directly and traceably from a mineral's own name, comparable in kind (though more precisely documented and dated) to the beryl-to-*Brille* etymological chain already established in this project's aquamarine entry.
**Primary historic sources:** Castleton, Derbyshire, England (the unique source of "Blue John," the most famous banded variety, found nowhere else in commercially significant quantity until a recent and much smaller Chinese deposit was identified); historical Persia/Iran (the source most strongly associated by Pliny and other Roman writers with murrhine, per the ancient trade-route literature); China (multiple Bronze Age and later archaeological sites, plus a modern minor source of Blue John-resembling banded material in Zhejiang Province); the American Midwest (significant prehistoric Mississippian-culture carved fluorite artifacts, see below); modern major industrial sources include China, Mexico, and South Africa.

## Source Quality Summary

Fluorite/fluorspar presents one of the most genuinely and persistently contested identification problems encountered anywhere in this project -- not a case of modern fabrication layered over settled ancient fact, but a real, two-century-old, still-active scholarly dispute over the identity of one of the most celebrated luxury materials of the entire Roman Empire.

**The murrhine identification controversy.** Pliny the Elder's *Naturalis Historia*, Book 37, devotes extended and specific attention to *vasa murrina* -- vessels of a precious material he describes as displaying blended shades of purple and white, sourced from the eastern Parthian kingdom (Persia), introduced to Rome by Pompey the Great following his eastern military campaigns and triumphal procession (Pliny places this specifically at Pompey's triumph, with the first vessels dedicated to Jupiter Capitolinus). Pliny records staggering documented prices: a single small vessel holding only about three *sextarii* (roughly three pints) sold for 70 talents (a peer-reviewed and a Tier 2-3 secondary source both confirm this figure, with one citing 70,000 sesterces as an equivalent figure -- the talent/sesterce conversion in different secondary sources accounts for the apparent discrepancy rather than indicating contradictory primary evidence); a *trulla* (a ladle-like nested strainer vessel) reportedly cost 300 talents. Pliny ranks murrhine among the most precious substances known, with gold reportedly ranked only tenth in comparative value and silver twentieth. He records a specific, named anecdote: an unnamed ex-consul was so attached to his murrhine cup that he gnawed at its rim (multiple sources confirm this specific detail, though the consul himself is not named in the sources consulted).

The identification of this celebrated material with fluorite/fluorspar is a documented, named, dated, and still-unresolved scholarly question:

- The fluorite identification was first proposed in published form by Ignazio Paternò in 1781, then independently and more influentially by Roman lawyer Faustino Corsi in his 1830 pamphlet *De Vasi Murrini* (named individuals, named texts, named dates -- both confirmed across multiple academic sources, Tier 2).
- C.E.N. Bromehead added mineralogical support for the fluorspar identification in a "Mineralogical Note" later elaborated in the journal *Antiquity* (1952), but the ResearchGate academic source consulted directly states that even by 1952, "Bromehead was unable to cite any actual fluorspar vase of Roman date whose present whereabouts is known" beyond two specific vessels independently published by A. Loewental.
- Modern academic synthesis (the ResearchGate-hosted scholarly article, Tier 2) states the matter as a probability rather than a certainty: "There can be little doubt that the murrhine ware... consisted of fluorite (or calcium fluoride, CaF2), although other materials, including glass, have been suggested." A separate, more recent academic source (the Italian-language *Murrina Vasa* monograph by Dario Del Bufalo, L'Erma di Bretschneider, 2016, named author, named publisher, named date, Tier 2) explicitly lists the full historical roster of competing candidate identifications that have been proposed over the centuries: marble, alabaster, fluorite, glass, porcelain, shell, amber, myrrh, and jasper.
- Only two intact ancient fluorspar vessels survive and are securely identified today: the Barber Cup and the Crawford Cup, both held in the British Museum, both recovered from Roman-era grave goods near the Turkish/Syrian border on the former Persian-Roman trade route (Wikipedia, corroborated across multiple sources) -- a striking case where the *physical evidence* for the identification is essentially reduced to two surviving objects, even as the *literary* evidence (Pliny, Martial, Suetonius, Propertius, Juvenal -- all named classical authors cited across the academic sources consulted) is extensive and unambiguous about murrhine's fame and value.

This protocol's Tier system requires this entry to state plainly: the murrhine-fluorite identification is the leading and most widely repeated scholarly position, but it is explicitly **not** a settled Tier 1 fact -- it remains, by the academic literature's own characterization, a matter of "little doubt" rather than certainty, with a competing minority scholarly tradition (favoring agate, onyx-marble, or other candidates) that has never been fully resolved despite more than two centuries of dedicated study by named scholars. This entry treats the Roman material-culture and economic history of murrhine ware as Tier 1 (Pliny's own account, and the corroborating accounts of Martial, Suetonius, Propertius, and Juvenal, are all named primary literary sources describing a real and lavishly documented luxury good), while treating the *specific identification of murrhine as fluorite* as Tier 2-3: the leading scholarly hypothesis, well-argued and widely accepted, but not proven beyond the kind of doubt this protocol requires for a clean Tier 1 designation.

**The Derbyshire Blue John "Roman discovery" legend -- a second, more clearly resolved fabrication.** Distinct from (though frequently and confusingly conflated with, in popular retail sources) the genuine murrhine-identification debate, a separate and much more recent legend holds that the Romans specifically discovered and mined Blue John at Castleton, Derbyshire itself, supposedly while prospecting for lead ore, and that two Blue John vases were excavated at Pompeii as proof. This claim is repeated with near-total uniformity across retail and tourism sources (Blue John Cavern's own visitor-facing website, C W Sellors, Fellows, W Hamond, Peak District Online, and others). It does not survive scholarly scrutiny. Wikipedia's account, citing the specific historical origin of the legend, states plainly: "It is sometimes said that Blue John of Derbyshire was discovered by the Romans. However, the earliest source of this story is William Adam's 1843 book *Gem of the Peak*. It seems likely that Adam was attempting to add some mystique to the Derbyshire Blue John." A second independent source (Regency History, citing mineralogist and Blue John historian Trevor Ford's dedicated book *Derbyshire Blue John*) confirms directly: "there is no reference to the mining and use of Blue John before the 1760s," and that Ford "explores and debunks stories of Blue John ware being found in Roman Pompeii." The Peak District Online tourism source itself, to its credit, hedges honestly: "Certainly a blue/yellow fluorspar has been excavated from Roman burial sites but this doesn't necessarily make it Castleton Blue John... There is also some debate over whether or not Castleton Blue John urns or vases were excavated from Pompeii." **This is Tier 6: a documented, named, and dated 19th-century literary fabrication (William Adam, 1843), constructed specifically to lend antiquarian prestige to a material whose genuine documented history only begins in the 1760s**, and it should be treated with the same explicit corrective clarity already applied in this project to the Jūratė and Kastytis legend (1842, amber) and the Massachusetts Pilgrim chiastolite legend (andalusite entry) -- this entry, the Jūratė legend, and the chiastolite Massachusetts legend form a striking cluster of three separate 19th-century-fabricated-antiquity narratives uncovered across this project's recent research, all following the identical underlying pattern: a genuine, much more recently documented material tradition retroactively dressed in invented ancient or medieval prestige.

The genuine archaeological record for fluorite is, once these two fabrication/disputed-identification problems are set aside, extensive, peer-reviewed, and global in scope -- considerably richer than the popular retail literature's heavy focus on the Roman murrhine question would suggest.

## Geographical Survey

### Europe

**England -- Derbyshire Blue John, the genuine documented history** | Tier 1-2

Setting aside the fabricated Roman-discovery legend addressed above, Blue John's genuine, well-documented history begins in the mid-18th century. Multiple independent sources converge on a consistent account: the ornamental value of the distinctively banded purple-blue-yellow fluorite found uniquely at Treak Cliff, near Castleton, was discovered (or rediscovered for ornamental rather than purely industrial purposes) around 1750 by local lead miners, who had been working the Castleton lead deposits for centuries (mining records in the area are documented from 1280 onward, per Regency History) but had not previously exploited the fluorspar veins for decorative purposes. Within ten years of this rediscovery, sixteen mines were reportedly working the area to supply roughly thirty local firms turning the material into ornaments (Peak District Online, Tier 3-4 but internally consistent with the broader and more rigorously sourced timeline). The material became genuinely fashionable among the British aristocracy in the later 18th and 19th centuries, used for vases, columns, tables, fireplace ornaments, and other decorative objects in stately homes -- with the celebrated industrialist and craftsman Matthew Boulton (named individual, well-documented historical figure) credited with significantly popularizing and refining its decorative use in the late 18th century (C W Sellors, corroborated by general decorative-arts history).

The 18th-century English fluorite vessels were treated, per academic sources (ResearchGate, citing established conservation literature), with a specific preservation technique: soaking the fragile, easily-cleaved stone in hot colophane (pine) resin to stabilize it for vessel-making -- and this academic source explicitly notes "a similar process must have been used in antiquity," suggesting the Roman murrhine craftsmen (whoever they were, working whatever the material actually was) likely employed comparable resin-stabilization techniques, which in turn would explain Pliny's specific recorded observation that murrhine vessels imparted "a certain fragrance" -- a striking case where a documented modern craft technique illuminates and helps explain a puzzling ancient primary-source detail.

The name "Blue John" itself has two competing documented etymological explanations, neither definitively proven: a derivation from the French *bleu et jaune* ("blue and yellow," describing the stone's coloring), or alternatively from the Cornish language word *bleujenn* (Old Cornish *blodon*, meaning "flower" or "blossom"), introduced via Cornish miners who came to work the Derbyshire lead mines in the 1740s (Wikipedia, citing linguistic-historical sources for both candidate etymologies).

The deposit is genuinely and severely limited: Blue John occurs in commercially significant quantity nowhere else on Earth except beneath Treak Cliff at Castleton (with a recently identified, much smaller and chemically distinct banded fluorite source in Zhejiang Province, China, sometimes marketed using the Blue John name -- a modern instance of the same kind of "false provenance" problem already noted, on a smaller scale, in this project regarding Chinese material occasionally sold as authentic Castleton stone). Mining continues today only on a very small scale, seasonally, in the two remaining working mines (Blue John Cavern and Treak Cliff Cavern), both of which also operate as show caves for tourists.

**Persia (modern Iran) -- the genuine documented ancient source region (regardless of the precise mineral identity of murrhine)** | Tier 1-2

Independent of the unresolved fluorite-identification question, the geographic origin of the genuine ancient material the Romans called murrhine is well and specifically documented: Pliny and corroborating sources place the source firmly in the Parthian/Persian east, and the two surviving securely-identified ancient fluorspar vessels (the Barber Cup and Crawford Cup) were recovered from Roman-era grave goods specifically on the former Persian-Roman trade route near the Turkish/Syrian border -- physical, located, museum-housed evidence supporting at minimum a genuine and significant Persian-sourced luxury stone trade into Rome, whatever its exact mineralogical identity.

**Prehistoric Western and Central Europe** | Tier 1-2 (peer-reviewed archaeology)

A 2026 peer-reviewed survey published in the Springer Nature journal *Geoheritage* (named journal, named publication context, Tier 2) provides the most comprehensive and methodologically current synthesis available for this entry, explicitly framing fluorite as "a common heritage of humanity" with documented ornamental use stretching from the Upper Paleolithic to the present day. The same survey specifically documents fluorite ornamental use in the later Middle Neolithic of Belgium and France, and separately in the prehistoric Iberian Peninsula -- the latter representing a third documented prehistoric ornamental-material tradition in Iberia in this project, alongside the amber and (separately, in earlier project research) other materials already documented for that region. The survey candidly notes that "literature detailing its ancient ornamental use is scarce" relative to fluorite's apparent actual historical prevalence -- an honest acknowledgment of a real research gap, distinct from a fabrication problem, and itself useful context for why this mineral has accumulated more popular myth-making (the murrhine debate, the Blue John legend) relative to its volume of rigorous archaeological documentation compared to some other materials in this project.

### East Asia

**China -- the most precisely dated archaeological fluorite find in this entire project** | Tier 1 (peer-reviewed, named scientific methods)

A 2024 peer-reviewed study published in the Nature-family journal *npj Heritage Science* (named journal, Tier 1-2) documents a fluorite bead recovered from the Tianshanbeilu cemetery in Hami, eastern Xinjiang, northwest China -- described by the study's own authors as "the largest Bronze Age cemetery in eastern Xinjiang" and a key node connecting the Eurasian interior to the Hexi Corridor and onward to China's Central Plains. The bead was identified and dated using two named, specific, modern scientific analytical techniques -- micro-XRF (X-ray fluorescence) and Raman spectroscopy -- and is dated to approximately 1385-1256 BCE, explicitly described by the researchers as "the earliest record of such a fluorite bead in China." This level of named-method, peer-reviewed, narrowly-dated archaeological precision is comparable in quality to the best evidence available anywhere in this project (the Assur ziggurat amber beads' FT-IR analysis being the closest parallel already documented).

The same study traces a documented evolution in Chinese fluorite use across subsequent centuries: by the early Western Zhou dynasty period, fluorite beads recovered from elite burials are described as appearing in newly standardized barrel or biconical shapes (a documented stylistic/technological shift from the more varied forms found at earlier sites), and -- significantly -- Western Zhou fluorite-bearing sites are found "a considerable distance away from known fluorite deposits," in explicit contrast to earlier sites which were located near the mineral's natural geological sources. The study's own authors interpret this distributional shift as evidence for "the rise of long-distance trade and exchange networks facilitating the transport of fluorite across China" by this period -- a genuine, peer-reviewed, evidence-based trade-network finding structurally comparable to (though independently arrived at from) the documented trade-network evidence already established in this project for amber, frankincense, and myrrh.

**China -- later ornamental and carving tradition** | Tier 2-3

Multiple sources, of varying rigor, document a substantial and longstanding Chinese tradition of carving fluorite (sometimes called "fluorspar" or occasionally marketed using jade-adjacent terminology, given its sometimes-similar green coloration) into decorative objects, drinking vessels, and figurines, with surviving examples documented from at least the Qing dynasty period onward (a named Qing-dynasty-or-Republic-period carved fluorite figure group depicting the He He Erxian, "Twin Immortals of Harmony and Union" -- a recognized pair of folk-Daoist/Buddhist deities of marital harmony and good fortune -- is documented in a specific surviving collector's-market example, Tier 3-4 for this specific object but consistent with broader, more rigorously documented Chinese decorative stone-carving traditions). A separate, methodologically rigorous 2025 *npj Heritage Science* study on glass ornaments from the Shuo Gate port site in Zhejiang (10th-13th century CE) documents fluorite being deliberately used as an opacifying agent in glass manufacture specifically intended to imitate the visual appearance of jade -- a distinct and specific industrial/artisanal use case, supported by named compositional scientific analysis, demonstrating that fluorite's role in Chinese material culture extended beyond direct ornamental carving into glass technology as well.

**China -- general ornamental and carving stone use** | Tier 2-4

Fluorite's softness (4 on the Mohs scale) and frequent green coloration made it a documented and longstanding substitute or complement for jade in Chinese carving traditions, used for drinking vessels in multiple colors per several secondary sources (Nature's Treasures, Tier 3-4), consistent with the broader and far better documented Chinese cultural reverence for carved hardstone vessels generally.

### The Americas

**Mississippian Culture, North American Midwest** | Tier 2-3

Multiple sources document substantial fluorite use among pre-Columbian Mississippian-culture peoples of the American Midwest, with carved fluorite artifacts and beads recovered from Mound Builder archaeological contexts dating to approximately 900-1650 CE (Nature's Treasures source, Tier 3-4, though this dating and general find-pattern is consistent with the broader and more rigorously documented academic literature on Mississippian material culture generally, which extensively records the use of regionally significant minerals -- including fluorite from the rich Illinois-Kentucky fluorspar district -- in elite grave goods and ceremonial contexts). A named academic citation appearing in the *Geoheritage* survey's own bibliography -- Boles, S.L. (2012), "Fluorite: a mineral of importance in midcontinental prehistory" -- confirms this is a recognized subject of dedicated peer-reviewed archaeological scholarship, though the specific content of that paper was not directly consulted for this research pass and should be reviewed directly for a future, more detailed treatment of this regional tradition.

### All Other Regions

**No documented mythological tradition found** for Egypt (though the Wikipedia fluorite article notes the Latin terms *murrina*/*myrrhina* are used "in archeology, gemmology, classical studies, and Egyptology," suggesting fluorite or fluorite-identified material appears in Egyptological contexts in a manner this research pass did not fully trace), Mesopotamia, the Hebrew Bible, Islamic tradition, India, Japan, Korea, Sub-Saharan Africa, the Pacific, or Aboriginal Australia. Given the genuine global archaeological breadth already established for this material (Belgium, France, Iberia, China, North America, England, and the broader Persian/Near Eastern trade), these absences should be treated as probable genuine research gaps -- consistent with the *Geoheritage* survey's own honest acknowledgment that "literature detailing its ancient ornamental use is scarce" relative to the material's actual documented prevalence -- rather than confirmed nulls, and are flagged here for potential future targeted research rather than presented as settled absences.

## Thematic Analysis

### Recurring Themes

**1. The Luxury of Fragility** (Roman murrhine tradition specifically -- Tier 1 for the cultural fact, Tier 2-3 for the material identification)

Unlike most luxury stones documented in this project, whose value derives substantially from hardness and durability (diamond, sapphire, the various quartz varieties), the genuine and extensively documented Roman fascination with murrhine ware is explicitly and repeatedly framed, across multiple named ancient authors, around the material's fragility and consequent rarity of intact survival. Academic sources directly attribute murrhine's scarcity in the modern archaeological and museum record specifically to its "fragile nature" and crystalline structure, "enhancing its desirability" in antiquity precisely because pieces were so easily damaged or destroyed. If the fluorite identification is correct, this fragility has a clean and direct mineralogical explanation: fluorite's hardness of 4 and its perfect octahedral cleavage (a property directly related to its crystal structure) make it genuinely much more prone to chipping, cracking, and breaking than the harder quartz-family and corundum-family stones more typically prized for vessel-making, meaning that murrhine's documented Roman cultural value as the *single most expensive material category known to Pliny* (ranked above gold and silver) would be a value structure built specifically around conspicuous, fragile consumption -- objects whose very breakability was part of their prestige, since only the wealthiest could afford both to acquire and to risk losing such pieces. This is a structurally distinctive luxury-value pattern not yet seen elsewhere in this project.

**2. Mistaken or Contested Material Identity Across Multiple Eras** (Cross-temporal -- a structural rather than narrative theme)

This entry is unusual in that its central "tradition" is, in a real sense, the history of scholarly disagreement itself. From Pliny's own ancient uncertainty about murrhine's precise geological nature, through more than two centuries of competing modern identifications (agate, alabaster, glass, porcelain, shell, amber, myrrh, jasper, and fluorite all proposed by named scholars at different points), to the deliberate 19th-century fabrication of a *false* certainty (William Adam's invented Roman-discovery-of-Blue-John legend, manufacturing spurious antiquity precisely because the genuine murrhine-fluorite connection was already circulating as scholarly speculation by the 1830s-40s and presumably suggested a tempting narrative shortcut) -- this material's entire documented history is, more than almost any other entry in this project, a history of people across many centuries trying to pin down exactly what a famous and desirable substance actually *is*, with that very uncertainty itself becoming part of the documented record.

**3. The Flux -- Transformation and Flow** (Etymological and industrial -- Tier 1)

Both of fluorite's primary names encode a documented and literal industrial function: "fluorite," from Latin *fluere* ("to flow"), and the related English mining term "fluorspar," both directly reference the mineral's longstanding and genuinely important historical use as a metallurgical flux -- added to ores during smelting specifically because it lowers the melting point of the slag and causes it to flow more freely, separating more easily from the desired metal. This is a documented, named, practical industrial application with a directly transparent etymology, structurally similar in kind (a material named for what it literally and functionally does) to iron's celestial-origin naming pattern or copper's Cyprus-derived etymology already established elsewhere in this project, though here the named function is metallurgical-industrial rather than mythological-celestial.

**4. Light Made Visible -- Fluorescence** (Modern scientific, but directly and traceably mineral-derived -- Tier 1)

George Gabriel Stokes's 1852 coining of "fluorescence," directly and explicitly derived from "fluorite" after his study of the mineral's glow under ultraviolet light, parallels (in mechanism, precision of dating, and named individual) the beryl-to-*Brille*-to-"electricity"-style etymological chains already documented in this project for aquamarine and amber. Fluorite is therefore, alongside amber (Greek *electron* → "electricity") and beryl (→ *Brille*/spectacles → indirectly the broader optical-science vocabulary), a third documented case in this project of a mineral's name becoming the linguistic root of a major modern scientific concept -- a genuinely notable pattern across the project's accumulated entries worth flagging explicitly for the protocol's thematic framework.

### Theme Coherence Assessment

The fragility-as-luxury theme is well-documented for the Roman tradition specifically but is contingent on the murrhine-fluorite identification being correct, which this entry has deliberately and explicitly declined to treat as fully settled -- this theme should therefore be flagged in any LoreCodex design use as "strong if the identification holds, speculative if it does not."

The contested-identity theme is, unusually for this project, not a *problem to be resolved before analysis can proceed* but is itself the single most historically and analytically interesting fact about this material -- there is no other entry in this project where the central scholarly controversy is this well-documented, this long-running (240+ years and counting), or this honestly unresolved even by the best available modern academic sources.

**Dominant overarching theme:** Fluorite/fluorspar is the material of beautiful uncertainty -- a mineral whose ancient identity beneath one of antiquity's most lavishly documented and most expensive luxury goods remains genuinely contested by serious scholars to this day, whose most famous regional variety's supposed ancient pedigree was a deliberate 19th-century invention layered onto a genuinely recent (1750s) discovery, and whose own name has twice become foundational vocabulary for entirely new branches of physical science (metallurgical flux chemistry, and later, fluorescence) -- a stone defined, more than any other in this project, by the history of how people have tried, and frequently failed with great confidence, to know exactly what it is.

**Genuine outlier:**

The Tianshanbeilu Bronze Age bead is a clean, narrowly-dated, methodologically rigorous, named-method archaeological anchor sitting almost entirely outside the cloud of contested identification and 19th-century fabrication that dominates the rest of this entry -- a useful reminder, consistent with this protocol's broader findings across the project, that even materials with an unusually high fabrication-and-confusion burden in their popular literature can still have genuinely solid, well-dated, peer-reviewed archaeological anchors once the right sources are located.

## Fictional Tradition

**Dungeons & Dragons / Pathfinder:** Fluorite/fluorspar does not appear as a prominently named distinct material in standard D&D gem or material tables, likely owing to its low hardness making it gemologically unremarkable by the standards typically used to construct such tables (most D&D gem hierarchies emphasize hardness and rarity together; fluorite's combination of striking color/banding with genuine softness is an unusual profile that doesn't map cleanly onto typical fantasy-gem value tiers).

**Modern crystal healing:** Fluorite carries an extensive modern (predominantly late 20th-century onward) metaphysical-properties tradition -- commonly associated with mental clarity, "auric cleansing," and learning/concentration, per multiple retail sources (Nature's Treasures, Tier 4-5) -- alongside specific and unsupported claimed deity associations (Vac, the Hindu goddess of speech; Minerva, the Roman goddess of wisdom) that, consistent with the pattern already well-established throughout this project for modern crystal-retail attributions, lack any named primary ancient source connecting either deity to this specific mineral and should be treated as Tier 5-6 modern invention pending discovery of genuine supporting primary documentation.

## LoreCodex Notes

Fluorite/fluorspar offers SKILLd20 a genuinely distinctive design opportunity: a material whose real-world documented history is defined by uncertainty itself, rather than by a settled tradition later obscured by fabrication.

**The "luxury good of contested identity" hook is the single richest and most novel design element in this entire project to date.** No other entry researched so far presents a real, named, extensively primary-sourced ancient luxury good (Pliny's murrhine, ranked above gold) whose actual material composition remains a genuine, still-unresolved 240-year scholarly debate. For SKILLd20, this is directly and honestly usable: an in-world legendary material whose true nature is disputed even by in-world scholars, where named characters could hold and defend different competing identifications, mirrors exactly the real historical pattern (Paternò vs. the agate/glass partisans vs. Bromehead vs. the modern Del Bufalo synthesis) without requiring any invention at all.

**The fragility-as-prestige hook** is a distinctive luxury-value structure not otherwise present in this project's existing entries (compare red porphyry's institutional-monopoly-driven sumptuary value, or alexandrite's geological-rarity-driven value) -- a material whose conspicuous *breakability*, not its durability, is part of what made it desirable, is a genuinely different design logic worth preserving explicitly if fluorite or a fluorite-analog material is incorporated into SKILLd20's economy.

**The William Adam 1843 fabrication** is the third documented case in this project's recent research run of a clearly named, dated, single-author 19th-century invented-antiquity narrative (alongside the 1842 Jūratė legend and the conflated-chronology Massachusetts chiastolite legend), and is worth flagging to the design team as confirming a real and recurring historical pattern across this entire research effort: the 19th century, with its Romantic-nationalist and antiquarian-tourism incentives, appears to be an unusually productive period for the specific kind of fabricated-ancient-pedigree narrative this protocol exists to catch. This is worth considering as a candidate addition to the protocol's named fabrication archetypes -- "the 19th-Century Invented Antiquity" -- as a sibling category to the existing "Renaissance Myth Mistaken for Ancient" archetype, but specifically flagging the 19th century as a second, distinct, and now multiply-attested historical period of concentrated fabrication risk.

**The fluorescence/Stokes 1852 naming event**, alongside amber's *electron* and beryl's *Brille* chains, gives SKILLd20 a third clean real-world example of a material name becoming foundational modern scientific vocabulary -- useful if the LoreCodex wants to develop an explicit in-world "etymology and natural philosophy" thread connecting its magical materials system to an in-world scientific tradition, since all three real-world precedents are now documented and cross-referenceable within this project.

**No further unresolved Tier 6 fabrications beyond the two specifically identified (the Blue John Roman-discovery legend, and the unsupported Vac/Minerva deity-association claims) were found in the sources consulted for this entry.**
