# Augelite -- Lore Research Entry

## Mineralogical Identity

**Name:** Augelite (from Greek *augē* / αύγή, "lustre" or "brightness," referencing the mineral's distinctive pearly cleavage sheen -- not, as one source incorrectly claims, named after a person)
**Alt names:** Amfilatite (a superseded earlier name briefly used by Igelström in 1866 for material later reclassified as augelite -- see Source Quality Summary)
**Entry type:** Type 2 -- Modern scientific classification with no pre-modern tradition possible. Augelite was not described or named until 1868, making any claim of "ancient" or "traditional" use definitionally impossible. This entry is structurally similar to clinozoisite and is, additionally, an unusually clear case study in how quickly and thoroughly a recently-named mineral's basic factual record can become corrupted across retail and SEO-driven secondary sources within roughly 150 years -- a useful and cautionary contrast to this project's more typical fabrication cases, which usually involve embellishment of a genuine older tradition rather than confusion over recent, well-documented scientific history.
**Hardness:** 4.5-5 Mohs (one source states 5 flatly; the 4.5-5 range from the more rigorous specialist source is preferred)
**Crystal system:** Monoclinic (confirmed independently by Wikipedia and Celestial Earth Minerals); one specialist gemological retail source states orthorhombic, which appears to be an error inconsistent with the two more authoritative sources
**Chemical formula:** Al₂(PO₄)(OH)₃ -- a basic aluminum phosphate hydroxide, consistently confirmed across every source consulted
**Color range:** Colorless to white most commonly, with occasional pale shades of blue, green, yellow, pink, or rose; transparent to translucent; vitreous to pearly luster, the latter being the specific property that gave the mineral its name
**Primary historic/modern sources:** Västanå iron mine, Scania, Sweden (the confirmed type locality); Horrsjöberg quarry, Sweden (the site of the earlier, superseded "Amfilatite" material); White Mountain, Mono County, California, USA (first facetable gem material, 1935); Rapid Creek, Yukon, Canada (first reported 1989, long considered the source of the finest crystals); Ortega mine, northern Peru (gem-quality green crystals, 2006); also documented in Bolivia (particularly the Potosí department), Brazil, Japan, Austria, and additional United States localities (Maine, South Dakota, Connecticut, Nevada)

## Source Quality Summary

This entry is, structurally, less about historical myth and more about a live demonstration of exactly the kind of source contamination this protocol exists to catch -- and it is among the clearest and most checkable cases encountered in this project to date, because the entire factual record in question is recent (1868 onward), well-documented in principle, and yet has already fractured into multiple directly contradictory popular accounts within roughly 150 years, without the excuse of ancient transmission, translation, or oral-tradition drift that explains discrepancies in much older material entries.

**The discoverer/namer is misattributed across multiple sources, with one clear outlier.** Two independently authoritative sources -- Wikipedia and the specialist mineralogical reference Celestial Earth Minerals -- agree precisely and in corroborating detail: augelite was first described in 1868 by Swedish chemist and mineralogist **Christian Wilhelm Blomstrand**, based on material from the Västanå iron mine in Scania, Sweden, with the name derived from Greek *augē* (lustre/brightness) in direct reference to the mineral's pearly cleavage sheen. A third source (Fierce Lynx Designs) adds a corroborating and genuinely useful refinement rather than a contradiction: it identifies an earlier 1866 description by a different named Swedish mineralogist, **Lars Johan Igelström**, who described material from the nearby Horrsjöberg quarry under the now-superseded name "Amfilatite" -- material subsequently recognized as the same species and absorbed into Blomstrand's later, formally accepted 1868 naming. This two-stage history (an earlier, differently-named description later reclassified under the name that achieved official priority) is a genuine and plausible pattern in 19th-century mineralogy and is treated here as Tier 2, since it is corroborative rather than contradictory of the Blomstrand attribution.

Against this, a fourth source (SkyJems) names **Nils Adolf Erik Nordenskiöld** -- a real, well-documented, and historically significant Finnish-Swedish mineralogist and explorer (and, notably, a close relative of Nils Gustaf Nordenskiöld, the mineralogist already documented in this project's labradorite entry in connection with the 1834 alexandrite naming controversy) -- as augelite's describer in 1868. This is a direct factual contradiction of the Blomstrand attribution on the single most basic biographical fact in the entry, with no corroboration from any other source consulted, and should be treated as an error, very plausibly arising from a confusion between two different historically significant Swedish/Nordic mineralogists who share a surname and were active in roughly the same era and field.

A fifth source (Acharyaganesh) states that augelite "was named after the German professor of mineralogy Gustav Augel." **No corroboration for the existence of a mineralogist named "Gustav Augel" was found in any other source consulted, in standard biographical mineralogical reference works, or in the etymological record**, which is unanimous and specific (across Wikipedia, Celestial Earth Minerals, and the SkyJems encyclopedia, despite their disagreement on the describer's identity) in deriving the name from the Greek word for lustre/brightness rather than from any personal name. This claim should be treated as **Tier 6: an unsupported and almost certainly fabricated etymology**, structurally identical to other invented-eponym fabrication patterns already flagged in this project, and is a particularly clean example because the correct, well-documented, and consistent Greek etymology is readily available and directly contradicts the personal-name claim.

A sixth source (Riyo Gems) states augelite "was first discovered in the late 19th century in the Andes Mountains of Peru" and derives the name from a different Greek word, "augos," meaning "brightness." This is a significant factual error on the discovery location and date (the confirmed type locality and date are Sweden, 1868, not Peru in the "late 19th century" generally), though the underlying etymological *concept* (a Greek root meaning brightness) is directionally consistent with the correct *augē* etymology, suggesting this source may be a garbled or imprecisely researched paraphrase of the correct underlying fact rather than a wholly independent fabrication. The Peru detail is plausibly a conflation with the genuine and well-documented 2006 Ortega mine (Peru) gem-quality discovery -- a real, later, and geographically distinct event -- mistakenly merged with the original 1868 Swedish type description.

**This entry's most useful finding for the protocol is therefore not a single embellished myth but a documented, checkable, multi-source factual corruption cascade occurring over roughly 150 years for a mineral with no ancient tradition to begin with** -- direct evidence that this protocol's source-tiering discipline is necessary even for, and perhaps especially for, recently-named materials where retail and SEO-content sources have had limited incentive to verify basic facts against primary mineralogical literature, and where errors can propagate and compound across multiple independent retail sites without any of them citing each other or a shared authoritative origin.

The genuine, well-corroborated modern gemological/discovery timeline (set out in the Geographical Survey below) is treated as Tier 2, resting principally on the SkyJems encyclopedia entry and the GemRockAuctions chronology, which substantially agree with each other on the sequence and approximate dating of later 20th- and 21st-century gem-quality finds, even where they may diverge from each other or from Wikipedia/Celestial Earth Minerals on the foundational 1868 description.

## Geographical Survey

### Europe

**Sweden -- the genuine type locality and naming history** | Tier 2

As established in the Source Quality Summary, the best-corroborated account holds that Lars Johan Igelström first described material from the Horrsjöberg quarry in 1866 under the name "Amfilatite," and that Christian Wilhelm Blomstrand formally established the name "augelite" in 1868 based on specimens from the Västanå iron mine in Scania, deriving the name from the Greek word for lustre. This is a documented, dated, named-individual mineralogical description consistent with the standard pattern of 19th-century European mineral science already well-established elsewhere in this project (compare Werner's naming of labradorite, Agricola's naming of citrine, and the Blomstrand/Igelström/Nordenskiöld confusion cluster of Swedish and Nordic mineralogists active across multiple entries in this project).

### South America

**Bolivia -- the first well-characterized crystallized material** | Tier 2

British mineralogists George Thurland Prior and Leonard James Spencer, publishing in the *Mineralogical Magazine* (a named, real, and still-active peer-reviewed mineralogical journal), described well-defined crystallized augelite specimens from Bolivia in 1895, providing the first thorough crystallographic characterization of the species beyond the original Swedish type material. This is corroborated, with matching named individuals and approximate date, across multiple sources (Fierce Lynx Designs, GemRockAuctions) and should be treated as solidly Tier 2.

**Peru -- modern gem-quality discoveries** | Tier 2-3

Gem-quality, facetable, green augelite crystals were found at the Ortega mine in northern Peru in 2006 (GemRockAuctions, corroborated in general terms by the SkyJems encyclopedia's listing of Peru among the localities most consistently associated with gem-quality or near-gem-quality material). This is a real, comparatively recent, and well-documented modern mineralogical find, not an ancient or traditional one, and the Riyo Gems source's apparent conflation of this 2006 Peruvian find with the mineral's original 1868 Swedish discovery (see Source Quality Summary) should be specifically excluded from any LoreCodex treatment.

### North America

**United States -- California, 1935** | Tier 2

The first facetable augelite gem material was discovered in California, USA, in 1935, and reported by American mineralogist Dwight Moulton Lemmon -- a named individual, named year, and named publication context (GemRockAuctions). The specific locality is further refined by the Celestial Earth Minerals and SkyJems sources to White Mountain, Mono County, California, in a metamorphic context associated with lazulite-bearing quartzites. The mine supplying this material was reportedly depleted within the 1930s, making this an unusually short-lived and precisely bracketed source episode.

**Canada -- Yukon, 1989** | Tier 2

The first report of augelite from Rapid Creek in Yukon, Canada, came in 1989 (GemRockAuctions), and these crystals are described as having been considered the highest-quality augelite specimens for many years thereafter, with a named example (a miniature specimen in the Tim Blackwood Collection, photographed by mineral photographer Rob Lavinsky) specifically cited.

### All Other Regions

**No documented tradition found** for any region, and none would be expected: augelite's entire documented history, in every source consulted regardless of their disagreements on specific names and dates, begins no earlier than 1866 and is confined entirely to the technical literature of mineralogy, gemology, and mineral collecting. There is no claim, even in the least rigorous retail sources consulted, of any pre-19th-century use, name, or tradition for this mineral in any culture -- which is itself a point of relative honesty in the retail literature, in contrast to several other Type 2 entries in this project (compare Agni Manitite, where modern retailers did fabricate a spurious Sanskrit/ancient framing) where retailers were willing to invent deep antiquity for a recently-classified material. Augelite's retail sources confine their fabrications to corrupting the genuine recent scientific record (the misattributed describer, the invented eponym) rather than inventing a false ancient one -- a structurally distinct and arguably less severe category of fabrication.

## Thematic Analysis

### Recurring Themes

There is no genuine cross-cultural mythological theme to analyze for this material, consistent with its status as a Type 2 entry. The available "theme," such as it is, is structural and methodological rather than narrative.

**1. The Self-Corrupting Recent Record** (The defining and only substantive theme of this entry)

Every other Type 2 entry in this project (clinozoisite, in particular) has demonstrated a *clean absence* of tradition -- the historical record is simply empty, and retail sources, to their credit in those cases, generally do not attempt to fabricate a false antiquity for a mineral whose 19th-century naming date is itself well and consistently documented. Augelite is different and, for the protocol's purposes, more instructive: here the *recent, well-documented, scientifically uncontroversial* record itself has been corrupted across multiple independent retail sources, producing at least four mutually incompatible claims about who first described the mineral (Igelström, Blomstrand, Nordenskiöld, or the apparently fictional "Gustav Augel") and two incompatible accounts of its etymology (Greek for "lustre," a different transliterated Greek term for "brightness," or a fabricated personal surname). This demonstrates that source contamination in the modern retail/SEO mineralogical content ecosystem is not limited to the embellishment of genuinely ancient or thinly-documented traditions -- it can and does also actively corrupt comparatively recent, well-documented, and easily-verifiable scientific history, seemingly through a combination of imprecise paraphrasing, confusion between similarly-named historical individuals, and at least one apparently invented detail with no traceable source at all.

**2. Optical Clarity and Restrained Beauty** (The genuine, modern, non-mythological aesthetic theme)

Insofar as augelite has any consistent characterization across the more reliable sources, it is aesthetic and technical rather than symbolic: the SkyJems encyclopedia entry specifically and accurately notes that faceted augelite has low dispersion (little "fire," the rainbow-flash effect prized in diamonds and some other gems) and that its appeal instead lies in "water-clear transparency and the intellectual satisfaction of owning" a genuinely rare, technically demanding collector's material. This is an honestly modern, collector-market-driven aesthetic appreciation, openly framed as such by the source rather than dressed in invented historical or spiritual significance, and stands as a useful contrast to the more typical pattern in this project of retail sources reaching for unsupported ancient or spiritual framing.

### Theme Coherence Assessment

There is no coherence to assess in the traditional sense used elsewhere in this project, since there is no genuine cross-cultural tradition present. The coherence question that *does* apply here is methodological: how consistently do the available sources agree on the verifiable facts of augelite's recent scientific history -- and the answer, demonstrated above, is "poorly," which is itself the entry's primary finding.

**Dominant overarching theme:** Augelite is the mineral of recent, real, and recently-corrupted history -- a material whose entire documented existence falls within the era of careful, named, peer-reviewed mineralogical science, and which nonetheless demonstrates, within that short and well-documented span, the same pattern of source drift, misattribution, and at least one apparent outright fabrication that this protocol more typically encounters when sorting through millennia of mythological accretion.

## LoreCodex Notes

Augelite offers limited direct narrative design material for SKILLd20 -- consistent with its Type 2 status, there is no ancient tradition to draw on, invent around, or correct -- but it offers unusually clear and well-documented value as a **protocol case study and teaching example**.

**The clearest documented case in this project of recent-history source corruption, distinct from ancient-myth embellishment.** Every other Type 2 entry (clinozoisite especially) demonstrates that retail sources, faced with a genuinely empty ancient record, will either honestly report the absence or fabricate a *new* ancient-sounding tradition from whole cloth (compare Agni Manitite). Augelite demonstrates a third pattern: retail sources faced with a genuinely well-documented *recent* record will sometimes simply get the recent, checkable facts wrong -- through imprecise paraphrasing, confusion between similarly-named historical figures, or invention of a plausible-sounding but unsupported detail (the "Gustav Augel" eponym) -- without any of the excuses (oral transmission, translation drift, genuine ancient ambiguity) that explain similar confusion in older material. This is valuable for the protocol's own self-understanding: source contamination is not solely a problem of antiquity, and the Tier system's discipline of requiring named, dated, cross-corroborated sources is necessary even for materials whose entire history sits within the last 160 years.

**For SKILLd20 design purposes specifically:** augelite is best treated, if used at all, as a rare specialist/collector material in the Materials system -- a mineral valued by in-world scholars, alchemists, or jewelers specifically *for* its rarity and technical/aesthetic interest rather than for any attributed magical, medicinal, or symbolic property, since none is supported by the documented record. If the design team wants an in-world parallel to this entry's most interesting real finding, an in-world "scholar's dispute over who first identified and correctly named a recently-discovered material" -- with one faction's claim resting on an invented or unverifiable source -- is a clean, low-stakes, directly transferable narrative device drawn straight from this entry's actual research process.

**No further action needed beyond flagging the "Gustav Augel" claim for explicit exclusion from any LoreCodex content**, and noting the Blomstrand/Nordenskiöld misattribution as a second, lower-severity correction for the design team's awareness.
