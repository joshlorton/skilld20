# Libyan Desert Glass -- Lore Research Entry

## Mineralogical Identity

**Name:** Libyan Desert Glass (LDG); also Great Sand Sea Glass
**Alt names:** "The Rock of God" -- a name that appears consistently across modern retail and popular sources attributed to "the ancient Egyptians," but which is **not traced to any named ancient Egyptian text, inscription, or named Egyptological primary source in any source consulted**; treat with caution (see Source Quality Summary)
**Entry type:** Type 1/2 hybrid -- A real tektite-type natural glass with one securely documented, named, and unusually precisely dated ancient artifact (the Tutankhamun pectoral scarab), explicitly described in the best-sourced literature as the *only* known ancient Egyptian use of this material, set against a substantial popular literature that incorrectly multiplies this single artifact into a broader and unsupported "ancient Egyptian tradition" of beads, jewelry, weapons, and religious naming.
**Hardness:** Approximately 6 Mohs (typical of natural silica glass)
**Material classification:** A natural glass, classified as a tektite or tektite-like impact glass -- among the purest natural silica glass ever found on Earth, with one named source giving a silica content of approximately 98% (arXiv preprint citing Wright 1999). Color is typically yellow to yellow-green, ranging from highly clear/translucent to milky, and may contain tiny bubbles, white wisps, or inky black swirls (ZME Science). Most pieces fall between roughly 0.75-1.5 inches (2.5-3.5 cm), per the Mini Museum source.
**Formation date and mechanism -- genuinely and recently resolved by peer-reviewed science:** Multiple sources converge on an age of approximately 28.5-29 million years (one Discover Magazine/Forbes source rounds to "29 million," ZME Science to "29 million," the Mini Museum to "28,500,000," Atlas Obscura to "20-million" -- this last figure is the clear outlier among otherwise tightly clustered estimates and is very likely either a typographical/rounding error or a confusion with a different dating reference, and should not be relied upon). The key unresolved scientific question for over a century was *how* LDG formed, given that **no impact crater has ever been identified in the strewn field** -- a genuinely unusual situation for a confirmed impact-glass tektite. This was substantially resolved by a named, peer-reviewed 2019 study: geologist Aaron Cavosie of Curtin University, published in the journal *Geology*, identified preserved zircon grains within LDG samples that showed crystallographic evidence of having transformed from reidite -- a mineral that, per the study, can only form under the extreme pressures of a meteorite strike. This is described in the Archaeology Magazine source as "the first bulletproof evidence that Libyan Desert glass was formed by a meteorite impact," resolving (or substantially advancing the resolution of) a long-standing debate between an impact-crater origin and a lower-pressure atmospheric airburst origin. The absence of a crater itself remains explained, per Cavosie, by the possibility that any resulting crater "may lie beneath shifting dunes or may have eroded to such a degree that it's no longer distinguishable on the landscape" -- a named, dated, and specific scientific resolution, comparable in rigor to the genuinely resolved Nördlinger Ries identification documented for moldavite in this project's companion entry, though arrived at considerably later and via a different and more contested research history.
**Primary historic/modern source:** The Great Sand Sea, in the remote southwestern corner of Egypt's Western (Libyan) Desert, north of the Gilf Kebir Plateau, on the Egyptian-Libyan frontier -- described across multiple sources as one of the most isolated and barren regions on Earth. The strewn field covers an area variously described as "tens of square kilometers" (Crystals For Days) or, per a separate estimate, distributed across a wider area with "over a thousand tons" of material present (ZME Science) to "about 1400 tonnes" (arXiv, citing Wright 1999) -- these figures are broadly consistent with each other and should be treated as Tier 2-3 estimates rather than precise measurements.

## Source Quality Summary

Libyan Desert Glass presents one of the clearest and most checkable cases in this project of a single, genuinely extraordinary, named, and precisely dated archaeological fact -- the Tutankhamun pectoral scarab -- being retroactively and unsupportedly multiplied by popular retail literature into a broader, unsupported "ancient Egyptian tradition" that the best-sourced literature directly and explicitly contradicts.

**The Tutankhamun identification itself is exceptionally well-documented, named, and dated, with one minor date discrepancy.** Multiple independent and credible sources -- the Mini Museum (citing two named peer-reviewed papers: Fröhlich et al. 2013 and Kleinmann, Horn, and Langenhorst 2001, both published in the journal *Meteoritics & Planetary Science*), Forbes (citing geologist David Bressan), Discover Magazine, and Atlas Obscura -- converge on the same core account: Italian mineralogist **Vincenzo de Michele** examined the centerpiece scarab of Tutankhamun's pectoral (originally identified by tomb-discoverer Howard Carter in 1922 as chalcedony, a form of quartz) and determined it was in fact Libyan Desert Glass. Sources differ on the precise year of this identification -- the Mini Museum and a parallel Mini Museum blog post both state **1997**, while Atlas Obscura and the original Forbes reporting state **1998** -- a minor, likely citation-rounding discrepancy comparable to similar single-year date conflicts already documented elsewhere in this project (compare moldavite's 1786-vs-1787 discrepancy), and not consequential enough to affect the entry's reliability. **This is Tier 1-2: a named individual, a named and closely-bracketed date, and a documented, peer-reviewed analytical basis** (optical measurement in the original identification per one source; later confirmed and extended by additional peer-reviewed compositional and isotopic work).

**Critically, the best-sourced literature explicitly states this is the *only* known ancient Egyptian use of the material.** The Mini Museum source states directly: "This 18th Dynasty find is unique among the gems of ancient Egypt, as it is the only known use of Libyan Glass." This same precise phrasing appears verbatim across multiple Mini Museum-affiliated sources consulted, suggesting a single well-researched origin point for this specific and important qualifying claim, and it is corroborated in substance (if not verbatim) by the Forbes source's note that "it's also unclear how the desert glass became part of Tutankhamen's treasures" and that "it remains the only known example where an Egyptian artist used this mysterious material."

**This directly and specifically contradicts a cluster of other retail sources.** Geology Science states that "Ancient Egyptians used it to craft jewelry, including beads, pendants, and other ornaments, which have been found in archaeological sites dating back to as early as the Middle Kingdom (around 2000-1700 BC)," and separately that "the ancient Egyptians referred to Libyan Desert Glass as 'the rock of god' and believed it to have mystical and protective properties," used "in the making of scarabs and amulets" (plural). Throwin Stones similarly states the material was "speculated to be worshipped by the pharaohs and their Gods in addition to its use as daily tools and weapons that were made from it," and that "by the ancient Egyptians use of this glass in King Tutankhamen's scarab pendant, it is discerned that it was regarded as 'the Rock of God'" -- note that this source's own phrasing ("it is discerned") signals an inference being drawn from the single Tutankhamun artifact rather than a separately documented naming tradition, which is a useful piece of self-evidence about how this fabrication likely originated.

**No named ancient Egyptian text, inscription, named excavation report for "Middle Kingdom" beads or pendants, or named Egyptological primary source corroborates any of these broader claims in any source consulted.** No source naming a specific Middle Kingdom site, a specific museum collection of LDG beads, or a specific hieroglyphic or textual attestation of "the rock of god" as an ancient Egyptian phrase was located. Given that the best-sourced and most specifically peer-reviewed-grounded literature (the Mini Museum's citation of Fröhlich et al. 2013 and Kleinmann et al. 2001) states plainly and repeatedly that the Tutankhamun scarab is the *only* known ancient Egyptian use of this material, the broader "Middle Kingdom jewelry tradition" and "the Rock of God" naming claims should be treated as **Tier 5-6: unsupported and very likely fabricated retroactive elaboration**, built by generalizing a single spectacular and genuinely unique artifact into an invented broader cultural tradition -- a fabrication mechanism worth naming explicitly for the protocol (see LoreCodex Notes).

**The Hypatia stone is a genuine, named, peer-reviewed, and remarkable adjacent find, distinct from LDG itself but found in the same strewn field.** In 1996, a roughly 30-gram, highly unusual carbon-rich pebble (~70% carbon by weight, with a hardness comparable to diamond) was discovered within the Libyan Desert Glass strewn field. Kramers et al. (2013), in a named and peer-reviewed study, named the stone "Hypatia" in honor of the 4th-century female philosopher and mathematician of Alexandria, Egypt -- a named, deliberate, and specifically documented act of scientific naming, not a folk tradition. Kramers et al. proposed that the same cometary airburst event responsible for Hypatia's parent body might also have produced the Libyan Desert Glass itself, a hypothesis subsequently and specifically critiqued by Reimold and Koeberl (2014, named authors, named rebuttal), with a later and more extensive isotopic re-analysis (the arXiv preprint consulted, presenting noble gas and nitrogen isotope data from laboratories in Nancy, France and Zürich, Switzerland) extending but not definitively resolving the question. This is treated as Tier 1-2 throughout: a genuinely contested but rigorously, repeatedly, and transparently peer-reviewed scientific debate, structurally similar in its open and named back-and-forth to the fluorspar/murrhine identification debate already documented as this project's clearest Type 5 case, though here the dispute is scientific/geological (what produced Hypatia and LDG) rather than historical/identificatory (what ancient material a name refers to).

## Geographical Survey

### Africa

**Egypt -- the singular and securely documented ancient use** | Tier 1-2

The entirety of this material's genuine, named, ancient documented use is the Tutankhamun pectoral, an 18th Dynasty (reign roughly 1332-1323 BCE) royal funerary object discovered by Howard Carter in 1922 and held today at the Egyptian Museum, Cairo (named museum, per the Mini Museum and Egypt Museum sources). The pectoral depicts the sun god Ra represented as a winged scarab beetle, carrying the solar barque bearing sun and moon across the sky, flanked by rearing cobras (uraei) and incorporating lotus and papyrus motifs representing Upper and Lower Egypt -- a richly described and well-documented object (Egypt Museum's own object catalogue entry, a named institutional source, provides this level of iconographic detail). The scarab was, per the Egyptologist commentary cited in the Discover Magazine source (Salima Ikram, American University in Cairo, and Aidan Dodson, University of Bristol -- two named, credentialed Egyptologists), understood by the ancient Egyptians within the broader and extensively documented symbolic framework of the scarab beetle (one of the most prevalent and well-attested symbols in Egyptian material culture generally, appearing "in all sorts of tombs beyond that of Tutankhamun," per Dodson) and within the pectoral's role, typical of such objects, in conveying religious protection and divine power to the wearer.

**How the raw material reached the royal workshop remains genuinely and explicitly unresolved.** The Forbes source states plainly: "It's also unclear how the desert glass became part of Tutankhamen's treasures. Archaeological evidence suggests that an ancient system of caravan routes existed around the Gilf Kebir Plateau, but it doesn't seem that the routes were used to search or trade for the desert glass. It seems that the piece used for the scarab was discovered by chance or maybe an exotic gift." This honest acknowledgment of genuine uncertainty -- not a fabricated explanation, but a clearly flagged open question -- is itself useful Tier 2 evidence of how the more rigorous sources in this entry's literature handle the limits of what is actually known, in direct contrast to the unsupported "rock of god" / "Middle Kingdom tradition" claims addressed in the Source Quality Summary. The Egyptologist Salima Ikram's comment, also cited in the Discover Magazine source, frames the find as evidence of the lengths to which Egyptians were willing to travel for rare materials: "It's remarkable because it shows us that the ancient Egyptians were making these explorations deep into the desert into places where even today, four-wheel drive could rarely make it, and they were doing it with donkeys" -- a named, quoted, and specific scholarly observation, though it stops short of explaining the specific mechanism of acquisition, consistent with the genuine uncertainty the same source acknowledges elsewhere.

**Prehistoric/Paleolithic use is separately and more broadly documented, and is not the same claim as the disputed "ancient Egyptian" claims above.** Multiple sources (Throwin Stones, ZME Science, the Mini Museum/Medium article) document that Neolithic and Paleolithic peoples in the region used Libyan Desert Glass to make sharp-edged tools, including blades, with the ZME Science source specifically noting this use predates the later Egyptian jewelry use ("Local inhabitants in the Neolithic period made tools out of the glass, and later the Egyptians used it to fashion jewelry" -- though this source's own framing of a broader Egyptian jewelry tradition beyond Tutankhamun should be read with the same caution applied above). The Mini Museum's Medium-published article adds useful environmental context: during the Early to Middle Paleolithic, the Great Sand Sea region "was often home to a wetter climate capable of supporting playa wetlands," meaning the area was not always the extreme desert it is today, and prehistoric peoples "coming and going as the climate changed" shaped the glass into tools and decorative items across a long span of pre-dynastic time. This prehistoric toolmaking use is treated as Tier 2-3 (consistent and unsurprising for a sharp-edged natural glass available as surface float, but not traced in the sources consulted to a single named excavation report or radiocarbon-dated assemblage).

**The 20th-century rediscovery is well-documented with named individuals and a specific, if slightly contested, date.** British (per most sources) or, per one source's more specific identification, British geographer **Patrick Clayton** visited the Great Sand Sea in 1932 and found unusual glass fragments resembling the Tutankhamun scarab material -- though he did not, at the time, connect the two finds; that connection was made decades later by de Michele. Multiple sources note that subsequent archaeological work identified additional Neolithic-era flakes of LDG at sites in the region, demonstrating, per the ResearchGate source consulted, that "Haji Hussein was not, after all, the first person to discover the glass" -- referencing an apparently named local individual or guide whose own discovery claim is referenced but not fully detailed in the source consulted, and who would merit further direct research if a more complete account of the glass's modern rediscovery history is wanted.

**No documented Roman, Greek, Islamic, or other historical-period Egyptian or North African tradition was found** beyond the single Pharaonic-era artifact and the separately documented prehistoric toolmaking use. This absence, across more than two and a half millennia of subsequent Egyptian and North African history between Tutankhamun's reign and the modern era, is itself notable and consistent with the "unique, singular find" framing established by the best-sourced literature -- if LDG had been a recognized and actively sought material in later Egyptian, Greco-Roman, or Islamic-era Egypt, a broader documented record would be expected, and none was found.

### All Other Regions

**No documented tradition found** for any region outside Egypt and the immediate Great Sand Sea locality. This is consistent with LDG's extremely narrow and remote geographic occurrence -- one of the most isolated source regions for any material researched in this project -- and with the apparent singularity of its one documented ancient cultural use.

## Thematic Analysis

### Recurring Themes

**1. The Singular Artifact, Multiplied by Retroactive Inference** (The defining and most analytically important theme of this entry)

This entry's single most important finding, structurally, is not a mythological theme in the conventional sense but a documented case of a real, extraordinary, well-sourced singular artifact being retroactively expanded by popular literature into an invented broader tradition. The genuine record -- one royal pectoral, one named identifying mineralogist, explicitly and repeatedly described in the best sources as unique -- has generated, in less careful secondary literature, claims of a Middle Kingdom bead-and-pendant tradition, a specific ancient Egyptian sacred name ("the Rock of God"), and general religious veneration "by the pharaohs and their Gods." The throwinstones.com source's own hedging language ("it is discerned that it was regarded as...") is, read carefully, an admission that this is an inference drawn from the single artifact rather than an independently documented naming tradition -- a uniquely clear piece of self-evidence for how this specific fabrication likely occurred, distinguishing it from fabrications elsewhere in this project that arrive fully formed with no visible seam.

**2. The Unexplained Journey** (Tier 1-2 -- an honestly preserved mystery rather than a filled-in gap)

Unlike many entries in this project where an ancient acquisition or trade story is either well-documented or has been fabricated to fill a gap, the genuinely best sources here preserve the gap honestly: how the raw glass reached the royal workshop, whether by deliberate desert expedition, chance discovery, or diplomatic gift, is explicitly stated to be unknown. This is methodologically a healthy example for the protocol -- a case where Tier 2 scholarly sources resist the temptation to manufacture a satisfying just-so story, in contrast to the fabricated "rock of god" naming addressed above.

**3. The Long Wait for Identification** (Tier 1-2)

A genuinely striking and well-documented theme: the scarab sat, misidentified as ordinary chalcedony, for 75 years after Carter's 1922 discovery before de Michele's chance viewing and re-identification. The Crystal Gemstone Shop source frames this directly: Carter's team's misidentification "probably contributed to the misidentification of the desert glass as chalcedony," in part because LDG, moldavite, obsidian, and chalcedony share broadly similar hardness and a family resemblance that has evidently misled observers more than once (this project's moldavite entry documents precisely this same confusion occurring in the opposite direction, with moldavite incorrectly credited with the Tutankhamun find by at least one source). The correction came not through systematic re-examination but, per multiple sources, by chance -- a mineralogist's passing observation in a museum gallery, decades after the original excavation.

### Theme Coherence Assessment

There is no genuine cross-cultural or multi-period theme to assess coherence for, since the entry's entire genuine ancient documentary record is a single artifact from a single reign. The coherence question that matters here is the same self-correcting, source-discipline question this protocol applies throughout: does the cluster of "ancient Egyptian tradition" claims in the less rigorous sources hold up against the more specifically peer-reviewed-grounded sources -- and the answer, as established above, is that it does not.

**Dominant overarching theme:** Libyan Desert Glass is the material of the singular, unrepeatable find -- a tektite so rare, so remote in its source location, and so narrowly attested in the ancient record that its one genuine documented use has, in less careful hands, been mistaken for the seed of an entire invented tradition, when the more honest and better-sourced reading is that ancient Egypt encountered this material exactly once, in one workshop, for one boy-king's burial treasure, and -- so far as the documented record shows -- never again.

**Genuine outlier:**

The Hypatia stone is the clearest outlier in the entry: a separate, scientifically remarkable, named, and still actively peer-reviewed-debated object found in the same strewn field, named in 2013 for an ancient philosopher but possessing no ancient cultural tradition of its own (it was not discovered, recognized, or used by any ancient culture so far as the documentary record shows -- it is a purely modern scientific find). It demonstrates that the Great Sand Sea strewn field continues to produce genuinely new, named, scientifically significant material more than two decades into the 21st century, a useful reminder that this entry's "ancient" record and its "ongoing scientific" record are very much still both active.

## LoreCodex Notes

Libyan Desert Glass offers SKILLd20 a genuinely distinctive design case: a real material whose one documented ancient use is spectacular, secure, and named in unusual detail, and whose design value comes precisely from resisting the temptation -- one that popular sources have not resisted -- to generalize that single use into a fuller tradition than the evidence supports.

**The "one royal object, full stop" hook is the strongest and most honest design element.** Rather than building out a broader in-world tradition for a fictional analog of this material, SKILLd20 could use the genuine historical shape directly: a singular, geologically and geographically remote material that one specific royal craftsperson, in one specific reign, used exactly once for one of the most significant objects in their entire material culture, with no broader precedent and no clear chain of supply -- a material whose rarity is not merely high but, so far as the documented record shows, was realized only a single time in the entirety of ancient Egyptian history. This is a different and more restrained design shape than nearly every other material in this project, most of which have at least a modest trade network or repeated-use pattern.

**The 75-year misidentification is a clean, named, dateable, and emotionally resonant design beat** -- a treasure correctly excavated but incorrectly understood for three-quarters of a century, corrected not by systematic re-investigation but by a single scholar's attentive glance. This is directly transferable as an in-world device: an artifact whose true material nature goes unrecognized by its own discoverers for a specific, long, and dramatically ironic span of time.

**The "unexplained journey" -- how the material reached the workshop -- is a genuinely preserved and acknowledged mystery rather than a gap this entry needed to fill with invention,** and is recommended as a direct model for in-world material lore: not every rare material's supply chain needs an invented answer; an honestly preserved "nobody knows, and the leading theories are educated guesses" is itself good and usable lore, modeled directly on how the best sources in this entry actually handle the question.

**Protocol note: this entry confirms a candidate new fabrication archetype worth naming explicitly: "The Singular Artifact, Generalized."** Distinct from the Reversed Antiquity Claim (aventurine) and the 19th-Century Invented Antiquity (fluorspar, amber, andalusite) archetypes already documented, this pattern involves a single, genuine, well-documented ancient artifact being retroactively expanded by less rigorous secondary sources into an invented broader cultural tradition -- additional named uses, an invented ancient name ("the Rock of God"), and a generalized religious-veneration claim -- without any second artifact, named text, or independent source to support the expansion. The diagnostic signature, observed directly in one source's own hedging language ("it is discerned that..."), is the visible seam between the one genuine fact and the inferential leap built on top of it. Recommended for addition to Section 9 of the protocol in the next revision.
