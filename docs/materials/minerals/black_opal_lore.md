# Black Opal -- Lore Research Entry

## Mineralogical Identity

**Name:** Black Opal (a precious opal variety defined by a dark body tone -- black, dark grey, dark blue, dark green, or dark brown -- against which the internal play-of-color appears especially vivid)
**Alt names:** None specific to the black variety beyond the general opal nomenclature; the Latin root *opalus* is itself debated (see Source Quality Summary)
**Entry type:** Type 1 -- Historical material, but structurally unusual in combining two almost entirely separate evidentiary tracks that must not be merged: (1) a genuine, named, peer-reviewed-adjacent Roman classical tradition for opal generally, predating black opal's specific commercial discovery by roughly 1,800 years; and (2) a modern, precisely dated, named-individual Australian discovery and Aboriginal Dreamtime tradition specific to the black opal variety and its primary source region. A third element -- the famous "unlucky opal" superstition -- is a documented and unusually precisely dated 19th-century literary fabrication that must be kept clearly separated from both of the genuine traditions.
**Hardness:** 5.5-6.5 Mohs (relatively soft; opal is amorphous hydrated silica, not a true crystalline mineral)
**Material classification:** Hydrated amorphous silica (SiOâ‚‚Â·nHâ‚‚O) containing up to 20% water by weight. The defining optical property, play-of-color (opalescence), results from the diffraction of light through a regular microscopic lattice of silica spheres within the stone -- a structural-color phenomenon (light interacting with physical structure rather than with pigment or trace-element absorption), distinguishing opal's color mechanism from every gemstone previously researched in this project, none of which produce color through this specific structural mechanism.
**Color and body-tone classification:** "Black opal" refers specifically to body tone, not necessarily to a literally black background -- the trade classification covers very dark grey through true black base material, with the play-of-color (often red, orange, green, blue, or violet flashes) appearing more saturated and vivid against the dark background than against the lighter body tones of white or crystal opal.
**Primary historic/modern sources:** Lightning Ridge, New South Wales, Australia (the dominant and most famous global source of fine black opal, essentially without rival); Mintabie, South Australia (a documented secondary black opal source, mined from the mid-1970s until closure, with the land subsequently returned to the local Indigenous community per the source consulted); historical (non-black) opal sources include Hungary/Slovakia (the dominant pre-Australian European source, see below) and modern Ethiopian deposits (a significant 21st-century source for opal generally, though not specifically for black opal).

## Source Quality Summary

Black opal's documentary record divides cleanly into three separable tracks that this entry treats independently rather than blending into a single undifferentiated "opal lore," consistent with this protocol's general practice of not allowing adjacent-but-distinct traditions to contaminate one another's evidentiary tiers.

**Track one: the genuine Roman classical tradition, confirmed against the primary text itself.** Pliny the Elder's *Natural History*, Book 37, section 80 (consulted directly via the attalus.org Latin-to-English translation, a Tier 1 primary-source access point already used elsewhere in this project) does discuss opal, embedded within his broader treatment of beryls, and does state that opal displays "the more subtle fires of the *carbunculus*, the flashing purple of the amethyst and the sea-green tint of the *smaragdus*, all combined together in incredible brilliance" -- a passage paraphrased with minor and inconsistent wording variation across nearly every retail source consulted (compare: "the living fire of ruby, the glorious purple of the amethyst, and the sea-green of the emerald," "having a refulgent fire of the carbuncle... the glorious purple of amethyst, the sea green of emerald"), which is itself a useful diagnostic: the wide and inconsistent paraphrasing across sources, none of which match each other or the primary text exactly, indicates a passage being repeatedly re-translated or re-paraphrased from secondary compilations rather than consistently quoted from a single fixed translation -- a pattern worth flagging for the protocol generally when assessing how reliably a "Pliny said" claim has been transmitted.

**The Nonius/Mark Antony anecdote is genuinely present in Pliny** (section 80-81 of the same passage: "there still exists even today a precious stone of this variety which caused Antonius to outlaw a senator, Nonius, the son of the Nonius Struma who made the poet Catullus so indignant") -- this is Tier 1, directly confirmed against the primary text. However, **two specific embellishments appearing across multiple retail sources are not supported by the primary text and should be flagged:** (1) the claim that the opal was intended as a gift for Cleopatra appears in several sources (A&S Opals, Macs Opals) but does not appear in the Pliny passage consulted, and is not corroborated by any other named ancient source -- this is very likely a later romantic embellishment, structurally similar to other unsupported "for Cleopatra" attributions already flagged elsewhere in this project (compare the peridot entry's documented pattern of unrelated stones being retroactively linked to Cleopatra); (2) **the monetary value of the stone varies wildly and inconsistently across sources** -- 20,000 sesterces (National Opal Collection), 2,000,000 sesterces (Jewels and Chains; NIXIN Opal Jewelry), and 20,000,000 sesterces (Some Fun Facts About Opals) are all separately asserted, a hundredfold range that is itself strong evidence of uncontrolled retail-copy drift rather than a genuine, consistently transmitted historical figure; the primary Pliny text consulted does not appear to state a specific sesterces figure in the passage examined, suggesting this number may originate from a different edition, a translator's gloss, or a later secondary source not yet identified. **Treat the core Nonius/Antony anecdote as Tier 1 (confirmed in Pliny); treat the Cleopatra detail as Tier 5-6 (unsupported embellishment); treat any specific monetary figure as Tier 4-5 pending identification of a consistent primary source.**

**Track two: the Australian discovery and Aboriginal Dreamtime tradition, which must itself be handled with care regarding source specificity.** The commercial/European discovery chronology is comparatively well-corroborated: opal was officially discovered near Listowel Downs, Adavale, in Queensland in 1869 (Good Opal Co.); commercial mining began in New South Wales at White Cliffs in the late 1880s; Lightning Ridge's opal-bearing ground was not recognized until roughly a decade later, with the first shafts dug in 1905; Mintabie's black opal deposits were found in the mid-1970s. These dates are internally consistent across the two most detailed sources consulted (Good Opal Co.; Ferris Jewelry, which separately states European settlers "began to recognize the commercial potential of opal mining" from the 1850s onward, broadly compatible with the more specific 1869 official-discovery date if read as the beginning of awareness versus the date of the first formally documented find).

**The Aboriginal Dreamtime tradition is genuine, but the sourcing quality varies meaningfully between specific tellings, and this variation should be preserved rather than flattened.** The strongest-sourced version consulted is the Black Opal Direct account, which explicitly names its transmission chain: a specific (though not further named in the source) Kamilaroi Elder ("Aunty") from Lightning Ridge, with the story -- "How the Opal Came to Be," featuring the butterfly-spirit Pallah-Pallah -- presented in her own words as transmitted to the author. This is treated as Tier 3 under this protocol's standard for documented oral tradition with a named culture and an identified (if not fully named) individual transmission point, comparable to other Tier 3 oral-tradition citations elsewhere in this project (compare the Apache puberty ceremony and Māori paua mythology citations). The Aboriginal Opal Story source similarly names a specific people (the Yuwaalaraay, also rendered Yuwaaliyaay in a separate source) and a specific narrative involving the supreme spirit Bhiamie, his two wives, and a crocodile spirit named Gurria -- again a named culture and a specific, detailed narrative, though without a named individual transmission point in the source consulted.

**By contrast, several other retail sources present a generalized, unattributed "Rainbow Serpent created opal" narrative in nearly identical language across multiple independent websites** (Opal Galaxy; Jewellery World; Masterpiece Jewellery), without naming a specific people, a specific Elder, or a specific narrative beyond the general assertion that the Rainbow Serpent's sacred waters from the Great Artesian Basin "played a crucial role in opal formation." This generalized version is plausible as a broad characterization (the Rainbow Serpent is a genuinely and extensively documented pan-regional creator-being motif across many distinct Aboriginal Australian language groups and traditions, well beyond opal lore specifically) but the *specific* application to opal formation, repeated in near-identical phrasing across multiple commercial sites with no named community or individual source, shows the diagnostic signature of generalized retail content rather than independently sourced ethnography -- the same pattern flagged elsewhere in this project (compare the citrine "Merchant's Stone" pattern, or the generic "ancient Egyptian" attributions flagged in the Libyan Desert Glass entry). **Treat the named-community, named-or-identified-individual tellings (Yuwaalaraay/Kamilaroi, Lightning Ridge-specific) as Tier 3; treat the generalized, unattributed pan-Aboriginal "Rainbow Serpent caused opal" retail framing as Tier 4-5,** while noting explicitly -- as the Jewellery World source itself appropriately cautions -- that "there is no single pan-Aboriginal interpretation of opal types, and caution is necessary when applying broad meanings across diverse Indigenous cultures."

**Track three: the "unlucky opal" superstition is a documented, unusually precisely dated, and unusually well-corrected literary fabrication.** Every retail source consulted agrees on the core fact: Sir Walter Scott's 1829 novel *Anne of Geierstein* features a supernatural character (named Lady Hermione in most sources; one source calls her "the Baroness of Arnheim," a discrepancy in the sources' own internal consistency worth noting) whose magical opal hair ornament changes color with her moods and who dies or disintegrates after the stone is touched by holy water; the novel's popularity is consistently credited with attaching a bad-luck association to opal that did not exist in the prior literary or lapidary record. **This is corroborated at Tier 1-2 by Wikipedia's own citation of a genuinely fascinating piece of period evidence: an 1875 exchange in the journal *Notes and Queries*, in which Sir Henry Ponsonby (Queen Victoria's secretary) wrote in to ask for the origin of the superstition and received several different answers, none of which named Scott's novel** -- with one respondent, Sir John Piggot, instead crediting a different work, and citing gemmologist Charles Barbot's attribution to the influence of the medieval legend of Robert le Diable. This is a genuinely valuable and rare piece of near-contemporary historical evidence showing that even people writing only 46 years after the novel's publication were not unanimous that Scott was the origin -- a nuance entirely absent from the modern retail sources, which present the Scott attribution as simple and uncontested fact. **This entry treats the Scott attribution as the best-supported and most widely corroborated explanation (Tier 2, given the close temporal correlation between publication and the documented sales decline, and given that no scholarly source consulted offers a stronger competing explanation), while explicitly noting the genuine historical complexity the 1875 Notes and Queries exchange reveals** -- the popular narrative is more confident than the primary 19th-century evidence actually supports, even though it remains the leading and most coherent explanation available.

A skyjems.ca encyclopedia source (itself an unusually careful and self-aware entry, explicitly framing the superstition as "the historical curiosity it is" and providing useful corrective context) further confirms that Renaissance and 17th-century lapidary writers including Anselmus de Boodt and Robert Boyle treated opal as desirable, that the Hapsburg imperial collection and French royal jewels both held significant opal pieces, and that "there is no significant pre-1829 textual tradition of opal as unlucky in any of the major European languages" -- directly corroborating the Pliny-era and medieval material gathered elsewhere in this entry and reinforcing that the unlucky association is a genuine and bounded 19th-century phenomenon, not a deeper or older folk belief that Scott merely popularized.

**The Black Plague color-loss legend and the King Alfonso XII cursed-ring story are separate, later 19th-century elaborations layered onto the original Scott-derived superstition, not independent corroborating traditions.** The Black Plague claim (that opals were observed to lose their luster after a wearer's death, fueling plague-era fear) is asserted across multiple sources but is chronologically incoherent as commonly framed -- the Black Death occurred in the mid-14th century, roughly 475 years before Scott's novel, and no source consulted offers a named 14th-century or other pre-1829 text documenting this belief at the time it supposedly arose, making it far more likely a retroactive 19th- or 20th-century elaboration projected backward onto the plague era to lend the already-fabricated superstition a veneer of deeper antiquity -- structurally comparable to the 19th-Century Invented Antiquity archetype already documented elsewhere in this project, though here applied to a belief rather than a material's discovery narrative. The Alfonso XII story (a cursed opal ring passing through the king's family with successive fatalities, ending with the king's own death) is presented by the American Gem Society's own source with an important and honest caveat: "these deaths could have been attributed to the cholera epidemic at the time, but the legend of the cursed opal persists" -- a useful instance of a retail-adjacent source flagging its own legend's weak causal basis, comparable to other self-flagging sources already noted favorably elsewhere in this project. The diamond-trade-conspiracy explanation (that rival diamond merchants deliberately spread or amplified the opal-superstition to suppress a competing gemstone) appears across several sources as a contributing factor but is consistently hedged ("it is also said," "purportedly") rather than confirmed by any named primary documentary source, and should be treated as Tier 4-5: plausible given documented gemstone-trade competitive dynamics generally, but not independently verified here.

## Geographical Survey

### Australia

**Aboriginal Australia -- the deepest and most geographically specific tradition documented in this entry** | Tier 3 (named-community tellings); Tier 4-5 (generalized retail framing)

As established at length in the Source Quality Summary, the genuine documented Aboriginal tradition predates the 1869 official European discovery by a span the Good Opal Co. source describes as "over 60,000 years" of Indigenous Australian presence and oral history, "long before its 'official' discovery." Multiple named regional traditions exist independently across the major opal-producing areas -- Lightning Ridge/Wallangulla (Yuwaalaraay and Kamilaroi tellings), Andamooka, Coober Pedy, Mintabie, and the Queensland fields -- and the Jewellery World source's caution against assuming a single unified "pan-Aboriginal" opal mythology should be treated as methodologically correct and worth preserving explicitly in any LoreCodex use of this material, rather than collapsed into a single generic "the Aboriginal opal legend."

The Black Opal Direct telling (the Pallah-Pallah butterfly-spirit narrative, transmitted from a named-by-description Kamilaroi Elder of Lightning Ridge) and the Aboriginal Opal Story telling (the Bhiamie/Gurria narrative of the Yuwaalaraay people of the same Wallangulla/Lightning Ridge country) appear to be two genuinely distinct traditions associated with the same specific region rather than variants of a single story -- both are treated here as Tier 3, with the specific narrative content of each preserved separately rather than merged, consistent with the sources' own framing that "there are a number of stories and at least two from the Lightning Ridge region."

**European/commercial discovery and the Lightning Ridge industry** | Tier 2

The 1869 Queensland find, the 1880s White Cliffs mining commencement, the c. 1895-1905 recognition and shaft-digging at Lightning Ridge, and the mid-1970s Mintabie find are corroborated with reasonable internal consistency across the Good Opal Co. and Ferris Jewelry sources. Lightning Ridge's emergence as the world's preeminent black opal source, and its continued production of "some of the world's most coveted black opals" through to the present, is consistently and uncontroversially affirmed across every source consulted, with no significant competing claim or fabrication identified in this specific commercial-historical track.

### Europe

**Rome -- the genuine classical tradition, opal generally (not black opal specifically)** | Tier 1-2

As established in the Source Quality Summary, Pliny the Elder's *Natural History*, Book 37, provides the core genuine ancient source: opal praised for displaying the combined fire of multiple other prized gems, and the Nonius/Antony exile anecdote, both directly confirmed against the primary text. Note that **black opal specifically is not and could not be the subject of this Roman tradition** -- Pliny's source region for opal was almost certainly Hungary/the Carpathian region (the dominant pre-Australian European opal source, per the Forsythe Jewelers source's note that opals have been traded since sources "dating back to 4000 BC in what is now Slovakia" and that "Hungary... was the primary supplier of opals to Europe for centuries"), which produces predominantly white and crystal opal rather than the dark-body-tone material that defines black opal; **any LoreCodex content should therefore attribute the genuine Roman tradition to opal generally, not specifically to black opal**, which was not commercially known anywhere in the world until the Australian 19th-century discoveries documented above.

**Medieval Europe -- the "eye stone" tradition** | Tier 3-4

The juyoyo.com source states opal was called *ophthalmios* ("eye stone") in the Middle Ages and was believed to cure eye ailments and to render the wearer invisible, making it "a prized amulet for thieves and spies" -- a claim repeated in substance (the invisibility/thief association specifically) by the HowStuffWorks source as well, giving this at least two independent corroborating mentions, though neither traces to a single named medieval primary lapidary text in the sources consulted. The HowStuffWorks source separately notes a documented medieval folk practice of young women wearing opal in their hair "to ensure their hair color would never fade," again without a named primary citation. These are treated as Tier 3-4: plausible, multiply (if shallowly) corroborated medieval folk traditions, but not yet traced to a named lapidary author (compare Marbode or Hildegard, both on this protocol's standard checklist, neither of whom was confirmed in the sources consulted as discussing opal specifically -- this remains a gap worth flagging for direct future consultation).

**19th-century Britain -- the Scott superstition and its immediate cultural aftermath** | Tier 1-2 (the novel and the 1875 Notes and Queries exchange); Tier 4-5 (the popularized causal narrative)

Fully addressed in the Source Quality Summary. Worth adding here: Queen Victoria herself is documented (Antique Jewellers source) as having gifted opal jewelry to her own daughters during this same general period, which several sources offer as evidence that the superstition, while real and commercially damaging, did not achieve total cultural dominance even among the most visible figures of the era -- a useful corrective against overstating the superstition's reach, paralleling this protocol's general practice of noting when a documented fabrication's actual cultural penetration was narrower than its modern retelling implies.

### Middle East

**Arabia** | Tier 4-5

The HowStuffWorks source states "the Arabians believed that the colourful stone rained from heaven in flashes of lightning," without a named primary Arabic or Islamic text. This is plausible as a poetic folk etymology (opal's play-of-color genuinely does resemble flashes of light) but is not corroborated by any other source consulted and should be treated as Tier 4-5 pending a named primary source.

### Greece

**Ancient Greece** | Tier 4-5

The HowStuffWorks source states "the ancient Greeks believed the opal gave its owner the gift of prophecy," again without a named primary Greek text (no citation to a named author comparable to this project's standard Greek checklist sources -- Theophrastus, Herodotus, or others already established as reliable anchors elsewhere in this project). Treated as Tier 4-5 pending further sourcing.

### All Other Regions

**No documented tradition found** for Egypt, Mesopotamia, the Hebrew Bible, East Asia, South Asia, the Americas, or other Pacific traditions in the sources consulted, beyond the genuine Roman and (separately) Aboriginal Australian material already addressed.

## Thematic Analysis

### Recurring Themes

**1. Contained Multiplicity -- The Stone of Many Fires in One** (Dominant in the genuine ancient and modern traditions alike -- Tier 1-2)

This is the single most consistent and best-evidenced theme across this entry's genuine (non-fabricated) traditions, and it has a direct and unusually clean structural-optical basis: opal's defining physical property, play-of-color, genuinely is the simultaneous or sequentially shifting display of multiple distinct colors within a single stone, produced by light diffraction through its internal silica-sphere lattice. Pliny's description -- the ruby's fire, the amethyst's purple, the emerald's green, "all combined together" -- is not poetic exaggeration but a reasonably accurate lay description of a real optical phenomenon, and this theme recurs independently in the Aboriginal Pallah-Pallah narrative (a butterfly's "dazzling multi-coloured wings" as the originating image) and in the general modern characterization of opal as containing "a galaxy of color" (HowStuffWorks). Unlike alexandrite's dual-but-discrete color change (addressed in this project's alexandrite entry, where the stone is fully one color or fully another depending on light source), opal's multiplicity is genuinely simultaneous and continuous -- a structurally distinct optical phenomenon from anything else previously documented in this project, and one whose mythological reception across two entirely unconnected traditions (Roman and Aboriginal Australian) converges on the same observation: this is a stone that should, by ordinary expectation, be only one color, and is not.

**2. Moral Hazard and Possessive Danger** (Roman -- Tier 1; 19th-century European -- Tier 1-2 for the literary fact, Tier 4-5 for its supposed deeper antiquity)

Two entirely independent and chronologically distant traditions converge on a related but distinct theme: opal as a stone whose possession carries genuine personal risk, though the *mechanism* of that risk differs sharply between them. In Pliny's Nonius anecdote, the danger is political and social -- the stone is so desirable that its owner is destroyed not by the stone itself but by the covetousness of a powerful man (Pliny's own editorializing dismay, "for the sake of a gem!", is itself confirmed in the primary text and frames this explicitly as a story about human greed rather than supernatural malevolence in the stone). In the 19th-century Scott-derived superstition, by contrast, the danger is supernatural and intrinsic to the stone itself -- the opal actively causes misfortune to its wearer. These are genuinely different thematic structures despite both involving "opal plus danger," and this entry recommends they not be merged in any LoreCodex presentation: the Roman story is about human nature responding to a beautiful object; the 19th-century superstition is about the object itself being dangerous.

**3. Origin Through Creature-Transformation and Spirit-Activity** (Aboriginal Australian -- Tier 3)

The two named Lightning Ridge-region traditions documented in this entry both involve origin through the activity or transformation of a spirit-being closely associated with a specific animal or creature form -- Pallah-Pallah the butterfly-spirit in one telling, the crocodile-spirit Gurria and the supreme spirit Bhiamie in the other. This is broadly consistent with, though not necessarily reducible to, the much wider and well-documented pan-regional Rainbow Serpent creation-being tradition across Aboriginal Australia generally, while -- per the Jewellery World source's appropriate caution -- resisting the flattening of these specific, named, regionally-distinct stories into a single generic narrative.

### Theme Coherence Assessment

The "contained multiplicity" theme is exceptionally coherent and has the cleanest direct physical basis of any theme documented in this entry -- two unconnected cultures, separated by roughly two millennia and the entire breadth of the Indian Ocean and Eurasian landmass, independently arrived at strikingly similar descriptive language (multiple specific colors named individually, "combined" or "dazzling" together) when confronted with the same underlying optical phenomenon, a pattern consistent with this project's broader and repeated finding that physically obvious properties generate independently convergent description across unconnected cultures far more reliably than do attributed metaphysical properties.

The moral-hazard theme is coherent only at the level of broad category ("opal plus danger"); its internal mechanisms are genuinely incompatible and should not be merged.

**Dominant overarching theme:** Black opal -- and opal generally -- is the stone that should not, by the ordinary logic of gemstones, exist in the form it does: a material that holds many colors at once rather than committing to one, that was prized in antiquity, nearly destroyed commercially by a single 19th-century novel within a single generation, and that carries, in its country of primary modern origin, two entirely separate strata of meaning laid one atop the other without erasing each other -- a c. 60,000-year-or-more Indigenous Australian tradition of spirit-origin storytelling, and a commercial/colonial discovery and trade history beginning only in 1869, both genuinely and separately true of the same stone in the same ground.

**Genuine outlier:**

The 1875 *Notes and Queries* exchange is the most valuable single piece of evidence in this entry precisely because it is a documented case of contemporaries *already* being uncertain about a fabrication's true origin only 46 years after the fact -- a rare and useful glimpse into how quickly a literary invention's actual origin can become contested even among careful, engaged, near-contemporary observers, distinct from every other fabrication-origin case in this project, most of which were only identified and corrected by much later (often 20th- or 21st-century) scholarship.

## Fictional Tradition

**Dungeons & Dragons / Pathfinder:** Black opal appears in standard gem tables as a high-value ornamental stone, typically among the most valuable gem categories, consistent with its real-world rarity and price premium over other opal varieties. No distinctly named magical-property tradition specific to black opal (as opposed to gemstones generally) was confirmed in sources consulted.

**Popular culture:** The HowStuffWorks source notes the 2019 film *Uncut Gems* prominently features a black opal "going so far as to suggest that black opals possessed magical properties," and separately notes the 1980s band Opal (later renamed Mazzy Star) as a direct cultural reference. ## LoreCodex Notes

Black opal offers SKILLd20 an unusually rich design opportunity precisely because its genuine documentary record cleanly separates into layers that can be used independently or in deliberate tension with one another, rather than needing to be flattened into a single tradition.

**The "two true things, same ground" structure is the strongest and most distinctive design element in this entry.** A single specific physical location (the Lightning Ridge region) carries both a documented multi-millennia-deep Indigenous spirit-origin tradition and a comparatively recent, precisely dated commercial discovery and extraction history, with neither erasing or fully explaining the other. This is directly transferable to SKILLd20 world-building: an in-world material whose deepest cultural meaning and whose commercial/extraction history belong to two different communities operating on two different timescales over the same ground is a richer and more honest design template than collapsing the two into a single "ancient legend now mined by modern prospectors" narrative.

**The optical "contained multiplicity" hook is the cleanest physically-grounded design element**, with two independently convergent ancient descriptions (Roman and Aboriginal Australian) of the same real structural-color phenomenon, offering strong design grounding for any SKILLd20 material associated with illusion, multiplicity, shapeshifting, or the holding of several distinct potentials within one form.

**The unlucky-opal superstition is recommended as a deliberate teaching case for the protocol generally**, not merely an error to exclude: the 1875 *Notes and Queries* exchange demonstrates that fabrication-origin uncertainty is not solely a modern research problem but was already a live and documented concern within two generations of a fabrication's creation, which is a genuinely useful and rare data point for understanding how reliably (or unreliably) cultural memory tracks the true origin of even well-documented modern inventions.

**The Cleopatra/Nonius embellishment and the wildly inconsistent monetary figures are recommended for explicit exclusion or, if used, explicit flagging** -- this entry's direct consultation of the Pliny primary text confirms the core anecdote (Antony, Nonius, exile) while finding no support for the Cleopatra detail and no single reliable monetary figure, making this one of the cleaner cases in this project of a real ancient anecdote being verifiably over-elaborated by retail-copy drift across multiple independent and non-corroborating sources.

**Protocol note:** This entry's finding regarding the wide and mutually inconsistent monetary-figure variants for the Nonius opal (20,000 / 2,000,000 / 20,000,000 sesterces, a thousandfold spread across the extremes) is recommended as a useful general red-flag pattern for the protocol: when a specific ancient monetary or quantitative figure varies by more than an order of magnitude across otherwise broadly corroborating retail sources, this is itself diagnostic of uncontrolled copy-drift rather than a genuinely transmitted historical figure, and should prompt direct primary-source verification before any specific number is included in LoreCodex content.
