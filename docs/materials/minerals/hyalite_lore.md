# Hyalite (Müller's Glass / Oregon Opal) -- Lore Research Entry

## Mineralogical Identity

**Name:** Hyalite (from Greek ὕαλος / *hýalos*, "glass")
**Alt names:** Müller's Glass (after its named discoverer, see below); Water Opal (a name it shares, confusingly, with the gemologically distinct jelly/Mexican water opal already documented in this project's companion entry -- see Source Quality Summary for the critical distinction); Jalite (the Italian-language translation/variant of "hyalite"); Amiatite (an alternate name given by a separate named individual for material from a separate named locality, now superseded); Lava Glass (a descriptive name referencing its frequent occurrence in volcanic fumaroles); Bacon Opal, Candy Opal, Waterstone (modern informal trade nicknames for specific banded or visually distinctive sub-varieties)
**Entry type:** Type 2 -- A real mineraloid with an unusually precisely documented late-18th-century European discovery and naming history, and essentially no pre-modern cultural tradition; this entry is structurally similar to clinozoisite and augelite, already documented elsewhere in this project, in that its entire meaningful historical record begins with named individuals in the documented scientific era, with the partial and important exception of a genuinely interesting cluster of 19th-century American place-naming history (see Geographical Survey).
**Hardness:** 5.5-6 Mohs (Wikipedia); 5.5-6.5 Mohs given in some secondary sources -- a minor and inconsequential range discrepancy
**Material classification:** An amorphous, fully transparent to translucent form of silica (SiO2), formally classified in modern opal mineralogy as Opal-AN ("amorphous, network-structured"), distinguished technically from precious opal (Opal-AG, "amorphous, gel-like," which contains the larger, more regularly packed silica spheres responsible for true play-of-color). This is an important and well-documented technical distinction, confirmed via direct consultation of Mindat's mineralogical database: hyalite's internal structure comprises much smaller silica nanospheres than precious opal's, which is specifically why hyalite does not display play-of-color even though it shares the same broad chemical family as black, white, fire, and jelly opal. Contains approximately 3-8% water by weight, either as a silanol group or in molecular form (Wikipedia). Lacks a plane of cleavage and exhibits conchoidal fracture; has a globular and botryoidal crystal habit, occurring as crusts and rounded masses in cavities within volcanic and pegmatitic rock, where -- per Mindat's citation of the peer-reviewed mineralogical literature (Flörke et al., 1973) -- the silica deposits directly from the gas phase during volcanic activity.
**Critical distinguishing property -- fluorescence:** Hyalite frequently displays a strong green (occasionally yellow) fluorescence under short-wave ultraviolet light, caused by trace uranium content within the stone. Multiple sources confirm this is generally considered safe -- the Crystal-Skulls.com retail source and the OpalAuctions.com source both note the uranium content is far too small to pose a radiological hazard, with the OpalAuctions source specifically and helpfully clarifying that the fluorescence and the (separately marketed, gemologically distinct) play-of-color phenomenon found in precious opal are two entirely different optical mechanisms that should not be confused with one another.
**Primary historic/modern sources:** Valeč, Karlovy Vary District, Czech Republic (a well-documented and frequently cited modern collector locality); Monte Amiata, Tuscany, Italy (the locality for which the superseded "Amiatite" name was coined); also documented from Mexico, Poland, Hungary, Australia, Germany, Russia, Brazil, South Africa, and multiple sites in the United States, including a historically and toponymically significant occurrence in Montana (see Geographical Survey) and a commercially significant modern occurrence at Opal Butte, Oregon (see Geographical Survey).

## Source Quality Summary

Hyalite's documentary record is, in sharp contrast to the genuine but loosely-sourced pre-Columbian Mesoamerican tradition documented in this project's companion jelly opal entry, exceptionally precise, well-corroborated, and almost entirely confined to the named European scientific era -- making this entry, alongside augelite and clinozoisite, one of the cleaner Type 2 cases in this project, with the genuine added complexity of a documented and historically interesting cluster of American place-naming history layered on top of the core European mineralogical naming chronology.

**The naming chronology is unusually well-corroborated across multiple independent and high-quality sources, with only minor and easily reconciled discrepancies.** The single most authoritative and detailed source consulted, National Gem Lab, provides a complete and internally consistent naming genealogy: the mineral was formally named "Hyalite" by German geologist **Abraham Gottlob Werner** (1749-1817) in **1794**, from the Greek ὕαλος (hýalos, "cup" or "glass"), for its resemblance to glass -- a date and namer independently and precisely confirmed by Mindat's mineralogical database ("Named from ύαλος ('glass') from its appearance, by A.G. Werner in 1794") and by the Crystal Council's retail source, giving this specific claim Tier 1-2 confidence across three independent sources of varying type (an institutional mineralogical database, a specialist gemological reference, and a retail source), none of which appear to be directly copying one another's exact wording. **This is the same Abraham Gottlob Werner already documented as a major and recurring figure in this project's labradorite, citrine, andalusite, and augelite entries** -- a German mineralogist active at the Freiberg Mining Academy whose naming work spans an unusually large fraction of this project's late-18th-century mineral-naming chronology, and whose recurring appearance across so many independently researched entries is itself a useful confirmation of his genuine historical centrality to this period of European mineralogical science, rather than a coincidence or research artifact.

**The "Müller's Glass" name requires a specific and important correction relative to at least one retail source's claim.** The Crystal Council source states that Franz-Joseph Müller von Reichenstein "discovered this stone and chose to name it after himself." **This is contradicted, and should be corrected, by the more detailed and internally consistent National Gem Lab source, which states plainly that "Müller's Glass is called after its discoverer Franz-Joseph Müller von Reichenstein" -- i.e., the name was given to honor him, almost certainly by others (most plausibly Werner himself, or other contemporary mineralogists, in the standard period convention of naming a mineral or variety after the person credited with first identifying it), not self-applied by Müller.** This distinction matters: self-naming after oneself would be a notable historical oddity, while being honored with an eponym by one's peers is the standard and unremarkable convention seen repeatedly elsewhere in this project's mineralogical naming histories. Independent biographical research into Müller von Reichenstein (via Wikipedia, Britannica, Encyclopedia.com, and a dedicated History Atlas timeline source, all consulted directly for this entry) confirms he was a genuine, well-documented, and historically significant Austrian mineralogist and mining engineer (1740 or 1742 -- 1825 or 1826, sources differ on exact birth date) -- best known to the history of science for his independent 1782 discovery of the element later named tellurium, a discovery that went unrecognized until the chemist Martin Klaproth confirmed and formally named it in 1798. **No source consulted, including the directly-researched biographical sources on Müller von Reichenstein himself, mentions hyalite, opal, or any glass-like mineral discovery in connection with his documented career** -- his independently and thoroughly documented scientific legacy is entirely about tellurium and mining administration in Transylvania, Banat, and Tyrol, with no corroborating connection to hyalite found anywhere outside the gemological-retail sources that mention "Müller's Glass" as an alternate name. **This entry recommends treating the existence of the name "Müller's Glass" as Tier 2 (well-corroborated as a name in current use, confirmed by Wikipedia, Mindat, National Gem Lab, and multiple retail sources), while treating the specific claim that Müller von Reichenstein personally discovered hyalite as Tier 3-4 (plausible given his genuine mineralogical career and his documented work in mining-rich regions of the Habsburg Empire, but not independently corroborated by any source focused on his own biography rather than on hyalite specifically)** -- a meaningful and worthwhile distinction this protocol's standard practice requires preserving rather than collapsing into a single confident claim.

**A third superseded name, "Amiatite," is well-documented with a specific named individual, date, and locality, and usefully illustrates the ordinary mineralogical practice of locality-specific naming being later absorbed into a single standardized term.** National Gem Lab states this name was given in 1802 by Italian naturalist **Giorgio Santi** (1746-1822) for material found at Monte Amiata, Tuscany -- a named individual, a named and specific date, and a named locality, treated as Tier 2. The same source notes, with a small and explicitly flagged irregularity worth preserving rather than smoothing over, that "the name Hyalite has also been used as being fully a synonym of Axinite (Klaproth, 1797)" -- referencing Martin Klaproth, the same chemist responsible for confirming and naming tellurium from Müller von Reichenstein's work, here apparently applying "hyalite" terminology to an entirely different and chemically unrelated mineral (axinite, a calcium-aluminum borosilicate) in a now-obsolete usage. This is a minor but genuine historical naming irregularity, not a fabrication, and is noted here as a useful small illustration of how mineralogical terminology in this formative period was not always immediately or permanently standardized even among the leading named chemists and mineralogists of the era.

**No pre-modern cultural, religious, or mythological tradition was found for hyalite specifically in any source consulted.** This is the expected and unremarkable finding for a Type 2 material whose entire documented history begins with named 18th-century European scientific description, and is consistent with this project's prior treatment of clinozoisite and augelite. The modern metaphysical-property claims found in retail sources (OpalAuctions's "waterstone" hydration/crown-chakra framing; the Crystal Council's "intuition, serenity and overall energetic flow") are treated, per this protocol's standard practice, as Tier 5: plausible-sounding modern attributions with no traceable pre-20th-century source.

## Geographical Survey

### Europe

**Germany / the broader German-speaking mineralogical tradition -- the core naming chronology** | Tier 1-2

As established at length in the Source Quality Summary, this is the dominant and best-corroborated track in this entry: Werner's 1794 formal naming, Santi's 1802 "Amiatite" naming for the separate Tuscan locality, and the somewhat less certain but still well-corroborated "Müller's Glass" eponym. All three names are documented with named individuals and specific dates, a density of precise mineralogical-naming documentation comparable to this project's best-sourced naming chronologies elsewhere (compare labradorite's Werner/Nordenskiöld sequence and andalusite/chiastolite's documented naming inversion).

**Czech Republic -- Valeč** | Tier 2-3

Valeč, in the Karlovy Vary District, is confirmed across multiple sources (Mindat's locality database; the Blue Star Traders/Crystal-Skulls.com specimen-specific retail source) as a well-documented and frequently collected modern hyalite locality, with no claimed pre-modern cultural tradition attached to this specific locality in any source consulted.

### The Americas

**United States -- Montana: a genuine and well-documented 19th-century American place-naming tradition** | Tier 2-3

This is the most historically and narratively interesting geographic thread in this entry, and one this entry recommends be preserved and used distinctly from the core European naming chronology, since it represents a separate and independently documented act of place-naming rather than mineralogical species-naming. Per a detailed newspaper feature consulted directly for this entry (Bozeman Daily Chronicle), a U.S. geological survey expedition discovered hyalite deposits above the head wall of what is now Hyalite Canyon, in the mountains south of Bozeman, Montana, in **1882**, and named the adjacent peak (10,299 feet) Hyalite Peak directly after the mineral found there. The canyon itself was originally known as Middle Creek Canyon and was not formally renamed Hyalite Canyon until **November 7, 1928**, when area residents changed the name specifically to distinguish it from other "Middle Creek" place-names found elsewhere in the state -- a specific, dated, and locally-documented renaming event independent of and considerably later than the original 1882 discovery and peak-naming. This is treated as Tier 2-3: a named, dated, locally-sourced historical account from a credible regional newspaper, though not independently cross-verified against a formal geological-survey primary document in this research pass.

The same source provides genuinely useful descriptive and folk-naming detail: a Gallatin Gem Club member is quoted recalling the stone being informally called "spit rock" by a visitor, "aptly" describing "some of the opal trailing down volcanic rocks with milky deposits trapped within it," while other specimens are described as resembling "tiny transparent fountains, frozen for eternity" -- vivid local folk-descriptive language, treated as Tier 3-4 (a named local source, but informal and anecdotal rather than a documented broader cultural tradition), and notably consistent with the same "liquid held in stone" descriptive pattern already identified as the dominant theme of this project's companion jelly opal entry, here arising independently in a 20th-century American regional context entirely disconnected from the Mesoamerican tradition documented there -- a useful small confirmation that this descriptive pattern responds to a genuine, independently-observable physical property of translucent opal varieties generally, rather than being culturally specific to any one tradition.

A named geologist quoted in the same source, Stephan Custer of Montana State University, offers a notably candid and unromantic assessment: "the stone's value is purely in its local interest... just go to any jewelry store and ask to see the Hyalite jewelry" -- an honest, named, expert acknowledgment that, unlike the genuinely commercially significant Oregon material discussed below, Montana's Hyalite Peak material has not achieved broader gemological or commercial importance, a useful and appropriately modest counterweight to the more enthusiastic framing found in most retail sources for this material family.

**United States -- Oregon ("Oregon Opal")** | Tier 2 (commercial/mineralogical record); no pre-modern tradition found

Oregon is confirmed, via a peer-reviewed-adjacent named source (Mindat's citation of Kevin Lane Smith's 1988 article "Opals from Opal Butte, Oregon," published in *Gems & Gemology* 24(4), 229-236 -- a named author, a named and major gemological journal, a specific volume/issue/page citation, and a document independently confirmed to exist via a directly-located GIA-hosted PDF) as a commercially and gemologically significant modern hyalite source, with Opal Butte (Morrow County, in the Blue Mountain Range of northeastern Oregon) specifically named as the primary locality. Mindat's locality entry confirms hyalite and rainbow opal as "the most common gem-quality varieties" found there, with the rarer play-of-color varieties (contra luz, hydrophane, crystal opal) noted as "economically more important" despite their lower abundance. A separate modern retail source (Aurora Gems) corroborates the general locality picture and adds further named Oregon sites -- the Last Chance Mine near La Pine, sites within the Bend/Fort Rock District of the Deschutes National Forest, the Juniper Ridge Opal Mine, and the Priday Polka Dot Beds -- though this entry notes these are documented modern commercial/rockhounding localities rather than locations with any claimed pre-modern cultural tradition. **No source consulted claims any pre-Columbian Native American tradition specifically for hyalite or Oregon opal generally**, and this entry treats that absence as a genuine and notable finding rather than a research gap, consistent with this protocol's standing practice of flagging meaningful absences explicitly -- it stands in useful and instructive contrast to the well-documented (if differently sourced) pre-Columbian Mexican opal tradition established in this project's companion jelly opal and fire opal entries, despite Oregon and Mexico both being part of the broader Americas and both possessing well-documented indigenous lapidary and ornamental stoneworking traditions generally (compare this project's existing turquoise references via the companion entries' broader Mesoamerican-material research).

### All Other Regions

**No documented pre-modern cultural tradition found** for any other region in which hyalite occurs (Mexico, Poland, Hungary, Australia, Russia, Brazil, South Africa), consistent with this material's overall Type 2 classification. Hyalite's occurrence in some of the same Mexican volcanic regions documented as the genuine source of the pre-Columbian jelly/fire opal tradition in this project's companion entries does not, on the evidence gathered for this entry, extend that pre-Columbian tradition specifically to hyalite as a gemologically and structurally distinct sub-variety -- a distinction this entry recommends be preserved rather than assumed, given the real and documented structural difference (Opal-AN versus Opal-AG, and the corresponding presence or absence of play-of-color) between hyalite and the play-of-color-capable opal varieties that are the actual documented subject of the Gubelin citation in the companion jelly opal entry.

## Thematic Analysis

### Recurring Themes

**1. The Glass That Is Not Glass** (Tier 1-2, with a direct etymological and physical basis)

Hyalite's entire naming history, across every culture and era documented in this entry, organizes around the same single observation: this material looks like manufactured glass but is not. The Greek-derived scientific name (hyalos, "glass"), the German vernacular "Lava Glass," the Italian "Jalite," and the colloquial American "spit rock" description (resembling something poured or dripped rather than crystallized) all independently encode the same visual confusion -- a natural mineraloid so closely resembling an artificial, manufactured material that observers across three centuries and at least three language traditions reached for "glass" as the most immediate available descriptive category. This is the same general "visual mimicry generates naming" pattern documented extensively elsewhere in this project, here directed at an artificial rather than a natural or animal referent -- structurally comparable, in this specific respect, to aventurine's already-documented reversed naming chain (a mineral named after a man-made glass), though hyalite's naming runs in the more conventional direction (the mineral observed and described using existing glass vocabulary, rather than a specific named artificial product being the literal naming source).

**2. The Hidden Glow** (Tier 1-2, direct physical basis)

The fluorescence property -- invisible under ordinary light, dramatically and unexpectedly revealed under ultraviolet illumination -- is consistently and accurately described across every technical and retail source consulted, and represents a genuinely distinctive physical property not shared with the jelly/fire opal varieties documented in this project's companion entries (which may display play-of-color but do not characteristically fluoresce in this specific UV-activated manner, the property being specifically associated with hyalite's frequent trace uranium content). This is one of relatively few materials in this project whose defining property is genuinely invisible under ordinary historical observation conditions (pre-electric-light, pre-UV-lamp) -- meaning hyalite's most striking modern characteristic was very likely entirely unknown or unremarked-upon by any observer prior to the 20th-century availability of UV lamps as a casual collecting and identification tool, a genuinely unusual situation among this project's materials, most of which were valued historically for properties immediately visible to the naked eye.

**3. The Local Landmark, Not the Sacred Stone** (Tier 2-3, specific to the American material)

Where this project's companion jelly opal and fire opal entries document a stone embedded in pre-Columbian ceremonial and mosaic tradition, hyalite's most substantial cultural-geographic association documented in this entry -- the Montana place-naming history -- is civic and toponymic rather than religious or ceremonial: a mountain and a canyon named for a mineral found there, by a 19th-century survey expedition, later formally adjusted by local residents for practical disambiguation purposes. This is a genuinely different and more mundane category of "stone and place" relationship than most of this project's other entries document, and is worth preserving as a deliberate contrast case: not every documented material-and-landscape relationship in the historical record is mythological or sacred in character, and this entry's clearest finding is a useful example of the more ordinary, administrative, and civic-naming end of that spectrum.

### Theme Coherence Assessment

The "glass that is not glass" naming theme is exceptionally coherent and has the most direct possible etymological basis of any theme in this entry -- every name this material has carried across multiple languages and three centuries derives from the same single visual observation.

The hidden-glow theme is coherent and well-evidenced but is, importantly, a property whose cultural salience is almost entirely a 20th-century phenomenon (the era of accessible UV lamps), meaningfully distinguishing it from this project's many other materials whose defining mythological properties were available to direct, unaided historical observation.

**Dominant overarching theme:** Hyalite is the stone of late and limited recognition -- a material named carefully, precisely, and entirely within the documented scientific era by named European mineralogists, later given a scattering of American place-names by 19th-century surveyors and 20th-century local residents, and possessing a defining hidden property (its fluorescence) that was very likely invisible to and unremarked-upon by any human observer for the entire span of the material's geological existence until the relatively recent advent of accessible UV illumination -- the clearest example in this project of a material whose most distinctive characteristic is, in the most literal sense, a modern discovery rather than a rediscovery or reinterpretation of something anciently known.

**Genuine outlier:**

The Montana toponymic history is the clearest outlier in this entry: a real, named, dated, two-stage place-naming event (1882 discovery and peak-naming; 1928 formal canyon-renaming for practical disambiguation) that has nothing to do with the material's chemical, optical, or commercial properties and everything to do with ordinary American frontier-era survey and local civic naming practice -- a genuinely different category of "material history" than anything else documented in this entry or, for that matter, in most of this project's broader research to date.

**No significant fictional or gaming tradition was found for hyalite specifically.** It does not appear as a distinctly named material in D&D, Pathfinder, GURPS, or Tolkien/Middle-earth source material consulted, consistent with its status, even within the broader opal family, as a specialist collector's mineral rather than a widely recognized gem-trade name.

## LoreCodex Notes

Hyalite is the cleanest Type 2 case yet encountered in this project's opal-family research, and its design value for SKILLd20 lies primarily in two genuinely distinctive and well-documented properties rather than in any inherited mythological tradition.

**The hidden-fluorescence hook is the single strongest and most distinctive design element in this entry, and has no close parallel among this project's other opal-family entries.** A material that is visually unremarkable -- or even, per some sources, easily mistaken for ordinary glass or quartz -- under normal observation, but reveals a dramatic and otherwise completely invisible property under a specific and historically late-arriving condition (UV light), is a clean and physically accurate template for a SKILLd20 material associated with hidden truth, concealed magic, or properties that require a specific tool, ritual, or condition to reveal -- and, unusually for this protocol's design recommendations, this property genuinely was not available to pre-modern observation, meaning an honest in-world treatment of a hyalite-equivalent material could legitimately treat its defining property as a recent discovery within the setting's own internal history, exactly mirroring the real material's documented history.

**The Montana toponymic history is recommended as a clean, low-stakes worldbuilding template for ordinary (non-mythological) place-naming.** A mountain and a valley named for a mineral discovery, with a documented later civic renaming purely for practical disambiguation reasons (avoiding confusion with other similarly-named places), is a useful and realistic small-scale worldbuilding device distinct from this project's more common "sacred site" or "lost and rediscovered quarry" patterns -- not every material-and-place relationship in a well-built setting needs to be cosmically significant, and this entry's clearest finding offers a documented real-world model for the more mundane alternative.

**Source quality flag for the design team: the "Müller's Glass = self-named by Müller" claim found in one retail source should be excluded or corrected.** This entry's direct biographical research into Müller von Reichenstein found no corroborating connection between him and hyalite anywhere in sources focused on his own well-documented scientific career (which concerns tellurium, not opal), and the more detailed and internally consistent National Gem Lab source's framing (the name given *in honor of* him, presumably by colleagues) is both more historically plausible by the standard conventions of the period and better corroborated by this entry's broader research.

**This entry, together with the companion jelly opal entry, is recommended as a paired teaching case for the protocol generally:** two materials sharing the trade name "Water Opal," researched separately per this protocol's standard practice for resolving naming ambiguity (compare this project's prior three-way Conium/Cicuta/Tsuga "hemlock" disambiguation), reveal genuinely different documentary profiles -- one (jelly opal) anchored in a real if imprecisely-dated pre-Columbian cultural tradition strengthened considerably by a single strong named-gemologist citation; the other (hyalite) anchored in a precise, multi-stage, named European scientific naming chronology with no pre-modern cultural tradition at all, plus a distinct and unrelated cluster of 19th-century American civic place-naming history. Treating these as one undifferentiated "water opal" entry, as several retail sources do, would have actively obscured both of these genuinely different and individually well-evidenced historical pictures.
