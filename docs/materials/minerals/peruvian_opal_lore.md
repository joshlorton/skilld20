# Peruvian Opal (Andean Opal) -- Lore Research Entry

## Mineralogical Identity

**Name:** Peruvian Opal; also widely called Andean Opal
**Alt names:** Pink Andean Opal / Pink Peruvian Opal (the pink-bodied variety specifically); Blue Peruvian Opal (the more common blue-to-blue-green variety, frequently compared in retail language to "the color of the Caribbean Sea"); Peppermint Opal (an informal trade nickname for pink material containing black dendritic inclusions); Cactus Opal (a separate informal name, associated specifically with green-blue material from the named Queropal mine -- see Geographical Survey)
**Entry type:** Type 1/2 hybrid, with one specifically identified and central unverified claim requiring exclusion -- this is a real material with a precisely dated and well-corroborated **modern** commercial discovery (1993, see Source Quality Summary), but the pre-Columbian Inca cultural tradition repeated across nearly every source consulted for this entry is considerably less well-sourced than the equivalent traditions already documented in this project's companion jelly opal and fire opal entries, and the specific and near-universally repeated "national stone of Peru" claim could not be traced to any named governmental, legal, or institutional source in this entry's research, despite a dedicated search effort -- a finding this entry treats as significant and recommends be reflected clearly in any LoreCodex use of this material.
**Material classification:** Common opal (non-iridescent, lacking play-of-color), distinguishing it structurally from the precious opal varieties documented in this project's companion black opal, fire opal, jelly opal, boulder opal, and Ethiopian opal entries. Geology Science's technical description confirms Peruvian opal "lacks this structure" (the regular microscopic silica-sphere arrangement responsible for play-of-color) "and is typically amorphous in nature," a finding independently corroborated by Geology In's source ("Peruvian opals often lack the characteristic play-of-color") and by the SkyJems institutional-style source's precise technical framing: "the material is non-iridescent -- it shows body colour without play-of-colour." **Pink Peruvian opal's color derives specifically from manganese trace content** (confirmed by gem.agency's technical source); **blue Peruvian opal's color is documented across multiple sources as associated with the material's occurrence alongside copper deposits** (the OakRocks source specifically notes the historic Lily Mine produced opal "along with" chrysocolla-malachite, both copper minerals, at the same location -- though no source consulted gives a precise chemical mechanism for the blue coloration with the same specificity given for the pink variety's manganese content, and this entry recommends this specific gap be flagged for any future, more technically detailed research pass).
**Hardness:** 5.5-6.5 Mohs, consistent with opal generally; Geology Science gives a specific gravity range of 1.98-2.25
**Primary historic/modern source:** Exclusively the Andes Mountains of Peru. The single most consistently and specifically named source location across multiple independent sources is the **Lily Mine** (also called the Pisco Mine or Pisco Umay mine), in the Ica region of southern Peru -- a working copper mine, with opal recovered as a secondary by-product of copper extraction (confirmed independently by SkyJems, OakRocks, and Sacred Loom Peru). Additional named regions include Huanuco (central Peru, associated with blue and blue-green material per Geology Science) and the separately named **Queropal mine**, associated specifically with green-blue "cactus opal" (per the Minerals & Gems Pak source).

## Source Quality Summary

Peruvian opal presents a genuinely instructive case for this protocol: a real and modern-era well-documented commercial discovery, combined with an almost universally repeated but functionally unsourced pre-Columbian cultural tradition, and a specific and central "national stone" claim that this entry's dedicated research effort could not verify against any named primary source -- a pattern this entry recommends treating with particular care given how widely and confidently the claim is repeated.

**The modern commercial discovery is precisely dated and corroborated by two independent and mutually reinforcing sources with matching specific detail.** Both OakRocks and Sacred Loom Peru independently state that Peruvian blue and pink opal were discovered in **1993** at the Lily Mine (Pisco Umay), in the Ica region, specifically as a by-product of ongoing copper mining at the site -- with OakRocks adding the further specific and economically grounded detail that opal extraction at the site became commercially unviable as copper prices rose and "many of the copper mines were bought up by Chinese companies," redirecting the site's primary economic focus away from the gemstone by-product. This is treated as Tier 2: two independent sources, matching specific date and location, with one source providing additional and internally consistent economic context that is not contradicted by anything else in this entry's research. **This 1993 date is notably and almost suspiciously close to the well-documented 1993-1994 Ethiopian opal discovery sequence already established in this project's companion Ethiopian opal entry** -- both materials entering the international gem trade within the same narrow window, a genuinely interesting coincidence of timing that this entry flags explicitly but does not treat as evidence of any causal connection between the two discoveries, absent any source suggesting one.

**The pre-Columbian Inca tradition is repeated with high frequency but consistently lacks the kind of named-source citation this protocol's standard checklist would expect for a claim of this specificity and confidence.** Nine of the ten retail and informational sources consulted for this entry repeat, in close paraphrase of one another, a specific narrative: that pre-Columbian Inca civilization (sometimes specified as the Inca specifically, sometimes more vaguely as "ancient Andean cultures" or "indigenous Peruvian cultures") regarded opal, and particularly pink opal, as a sacred gift from **Pachamama**, the Andean earth-mother deity associated with fertility, used in ritual contexts to "communicate with deities or spirits." **No source consulted cites a named primary ethnohistorical text, a named Spanish colonial chronicler (the kind of named source -- comparable to Sahagún's Florentine Codex, already established as the relevant primary-source category for this kind of claim in this project's companion jelly opal and fire opal entries -- that would be expected for a genuine pre-Columbian Andean material-culture claim of this specificity), or a named modern ethnographic or archaeological study.** This stands in sharp and instructive contrast to the considerably better-sourced Mexican opal tradition already documented in this project (the Gubelin/*Australian Gemmologist* 1986 citation for jelly opal, and the Eckert/*The World of Opals* citation for fire opal) -- in both of those cases, this protocol was able to locate at least one named gemologist or scholar making a specific, citable claim. **No comparable named source was located for the Peruvian/Inca opal tradition in this entry's research**, despite a dedicated search effort.

**One source consulted provides a directly relevant and unusually honest methodological caveat that this entry treats as the single most important piece of evidence regarding this tradition's reliability.** Moonrise Crystals states plainly: "Very little is known about the religion and culture of the Incan Empire, but in the late 20th century, a new practice of worshiping the Incan goddess Pachamama has become popular in the Andes." This is a genuinely significant and self-undermining admission from within the same broader body of retail literature that elsewhere asserts confident pre-Columbian opal-Pachamama ritual connections: the source itself acknowledges that detailed knowledge of actual Inca-era religious practice is limited, while separately noting that **organized Pachamama veneration as practiced today is specifically a late-20th-century phenomenon**, not a continuously unbroken pre-Columbian survival. This directly parallels and reinforces a pattern already documented in this project's companion black opal entry's most important methodological finding (the 1875 *Notes and Queries* exchange revealing contemporary uncertainty about the Walter Scott superstition's true origin): **a source's own internal honesty about the limits of historical knowledge is itself valuable evidence**, and this entry treats Moonrise Crystals's caveat as the strongest single piece of source-quality evidence in this entry, even though the source's broader article does not consistently apply this same caution to its own opal-Pachamama claims elsewhere in the same piece.

**This entry recommends treating the Inca/Pachamama opal tradition as Tier 4-5: a widely repeated and culturally plausible claim** (Pachamama is a genuine, well-documented, and major figure in Andean religious tradition both historically and in ongoing practice, and an association between a beautiful local earth-mined stone and an earth-mother deity is not an implausible pairing in the abstract) **but one this entry could not trace to any named primary or scholarly secondary source specifically connecting opal to Pachamama**, as distinct from the broader and genuinely well-documented Pachamama tradition itself. This is a meaningfully different and more cautious tier assignment than the confident, unhedged "Incan civilization revered pink opal as a sacred stone" framing found in most of the retail sources consulted.

**The "national stone of Peru" claim is the single most consequential unverified claim in this entry and is recommended for explicit caution in any LoreCodex use.** This claim appears, in near-identical phrasing, across at least seven of the ten sources consulted (OpalAuctions, twice across two separate pages; Moonrise Crystals; Geology Science; Fire Mountain Gems; Geology In; Heart of Stone Studio; Laurella) -- a degree of cross-source repetition and phrasing similarity that this protocol has, in prior entries, identified as the diagnostic signature of homogenized retail content circulating without independent verification, rather than genuine and independently confirmed institutional fact (compare the "Mexican Diamond" and unverified "national gemstone of Mexico" claims already flagged with similar caution in this project's companion jelly opal entry). **This entry conducted a dedicated, separate search specifically attempting to locate a named Peruvian governmental decree, law, or official institutional designation establishing opal as a national stone or gemstone, and found none.** No source consulted -- including the otherwise more careful and institutionally-styled SkyJems source, which notably does *not* repeat the national-stone claim at all -- cites a specific law, decree number, government ministry, or dated official act. This is treated as **Tier 5: a widely repeated but functionally unverified claim**, and this entry specifically notes that SkyJems' conspicuous *omission* of this claim, despite covering Peruvian gem production in comparable depth to several of the sources that do make the claim, is itself mildly corroborating evidence that the claim does not rest on solid institutional ground -- a source that is otherwise comprehensive and detailed choosing not to repeat a claim that nine other, less careful sources repeat without hesitation is a meaningful absence, consistent with this protocol's standing practice of treating a careful source's silence on a widely-repeated claim as informative.

## Geographical Survey

### South America

**Peru -- the exclusive source, with the modern commercial history concentrated at one specifically named mine** | Tier 2 (modern discovery and commercial history); Tier 4-5 (pre-Columbian Inca tradition); Tier 5 (national stone designation)

As established at length above, this entry's strongest and most reliable documented history is recent, specific, and economically grounded: the 1993 discovery of gem-quality blue and pink opal as a secondary by-product of copper mining at the Lily Mine (Pisco Umay), Ica region, with the SkyJems source independently confirming and adding useful broader regional context: Peru's gem production generally is "a marginal by-product" of a mining sector "dominated by copper, gold, silver, and zinc," with Peru ranking among the world's largest producers of those base and precious metals -- meaning Peruvian opal's discovery as a copper-mining by-product is entirely consistent with, rather than an exception to, the broader and well-documented character of Peru's mining economy generally.

**The Queropal mine and "cactus opal"** | Tier 3

The Minerals & Gems Pak source separately names the Queropal mine as "the principal opal deposit in Peru" supplying "cactus opal," a green-blue variety -- a claim not directly corroborated by any other source consulted, though not contradicted either, and consistent in general character with the broader Andean opal picture established by the other, better-corroborated sources. This entry treats this as a genuine but singly-sourced detail, Tier 3, recommended for further verification rather than exclusion.

**Pre-Columbian Andean material culture more broadly** | Tier 1-2 (general context); not directly connected to opal specifically

SkyJems's broader Peru-focused source provides useful and well-grounded context for the genuine pre-Columbian Andean material-culture tradition, independent of the specific and unverified opal claims addressed above: documented Moche, Chavín, and Inca metalwork traditions, and Inca turquoise-inlaid artefacts, alongside the well-documented Inca cosmological association of gold with the Sun and silver with the Moon, which "informed the production and ceremonial use of metalwork at the imperial level." This is genuine, well-corroborated pre-Columbian Andean material-culture history -- but **it concerns metalwork and turquoise specifically, not opal**, and this entry recommends this distinction be preserved carefully: the existence of a rich, genuine, and well-documented pre-Columbian Andean material-culture tradition generally does not, by itself, constitute evidence for the specific and separate claim that opal in particular was part of that same documented tradition, any more than the existence of well-documented ancient Egyptian material culture would constitute evidence for an unverified claim about a specific Egyptian use of a specific stone absent its own direct sourcing (a distinction this protocol has applied consistently throughout this project's research, including most recently in the Libyan Desert Glass entry's treatment of "the Singular Artifact, Generalized" fabrication pattern).

### All Other Regions

**No documented tradition found** for any region outside Peru specifically regarding Peruvian/Andean opal. Pink opal is separately documented, per OpalAuctions, as also occurring in Mexico (Durango), the United States (Idaho and Oregon), and Western Australia (where it is technically opalized radiolarite rather than true opal, a useful and specifically noted mineralogical distinction) -- but no source consulted attaches any historical or cultural tradition to these other pink-opal-producing regions, and this entry's scope remains specifically Peruvian/Andean material.

## Thematic Analysis

### Recurring Themes

**1. The Earth Mother's Gift -- A Widely Asserted but Thinly Sourced Theme** (Tier 4-5)

This is the dominant theme across the retail literature for this material, and this entry's central methodological finding is that the theme's near-universal repetition should not be mistaken for strong sourcing. The pairing of an earth-toned, earth-mined stone with an earth-mother deity is thematically coherent and intuitive in the abstract (a pattern this protocol has noted elsewhere as a plausible basis for genuine independent cultural convergence, compare the visual-mimicry naming patterns already documented for fire opal's hummingbird association and labradorite's Aurora association) -- but unlike those better-sourced cases, this entry was unable to locate any named primary or scholarly source specifically connecting opal to Pachamama, as distinct from the broader and genuinely documented Pachamama tradition itself.

**2. By-Product of a Greater Industry** (Tier 2, well-corroborated)

The genuine and best-documented theme in this entry is economic and modern: Peruvian opal's discovery and continued production exist specifically as a secondary by-product of Peru's much larger and more economically significant copper mining industry, a relationship documented with unusual specificity (the named Lily Mine, the named 1993 date, the documented commercial deprioritization as copper prices and ownership patterns shifted). This is a genuinely distinctive economic-historical pattern relative to this project's other opal entries -- where Australian and Ethiopian opal mining are dedicated gemstone-extraction industries in their own right, Peruvian opal's modern history is one of incidental discovery within a mining operation focused on an entirely different and more economically significant material.

**3. Color Without Fire -- Calm as the Defining Aesthetic Property** (Tier 1-2, direct physical basis)

Every source consulted, regardless of evidentiary tier on cultural questions, agrees on the same core physical and aesthetic fact: Peruvian opal's appeal rests specifically on its *absence* of the dramatic play-of-color that defines precious opal varieties documented elsewhere in this project, in favor of calm, uniform, pastel body color. This is consistently and independently described using calm/tranquility language ("the tranquil waters of the Caribbean," "soothing colors," "gentle iridescence") across multiple unconnected sources, a pattern consistent with this project's repeated finding that genuine, directly observable physical properties generate convergent descriptive language more reliably than attributed cultural or spiritual properties do -- here, the descriptive convergence (calm, oceanic, soothing) tracks the stone's genuine and well-documented optical character (non-iridescent, pastel, lacking dramatic flash) rather than any specific cultural tradition.

### Theme Coherence Assessment

The "earth mother's gift" theme has wide repetition but weak individual sourcing -- a pattern this protocol has learned, across this project's broader research, to treat as a warning sign rather than as corroborating strength, since wide repetition of an unsourced claim across commercially-motivated retail content is generally evidence of content homogenization rather than independent verification (compare the near-identical "national stone" phrasing flagged above, and the broader pattern already established for several other materials in this project, including the Tutankhamun/moldavite conflation and the aventurine ancient-civilization cluster).

The by-product/secondary-industry theme is the most coherent and best-evidenced theme in this entry, resting on specific, named, mutually-corroborating modern sources.

**Dominant overarching theme:** Peruvian opal is the stone of confident repetition outrunning verification -- a material whose modern commercial history is genuinely well-documented and specific, but whose popular cultural narrative (the Pachamama gift tradition, the national stone designation) is asserted with a confidence this entry's dedicated research was unable to match with comparably specific sourcing, making this entry's most valuable contribution a demonstration of exactly the gap this protocol exists to identify between how confidently a claim is repeated and how well it is actually supported.

**Genuine outlier:**

The Moonrise Crystals source's own internal caveat -- acknowledging genuine limits on what is known about historical Inca religious practice, in the same article that elsewhere asserts confident opal-Pachamama connections -- is the most valuable single piece of evidence in this entry, precisely because it demonstrates a source partially undermining its own broader narrative through an isolated moment of genuine historical caution, a pattern this project has now documented in multiple entries (compare the 1875 *Notes and Queries* exchange in the black opal entry, and the Gubelin Cortés-inventory caveat in the jelly opal entry) and is recommended as a positive model: even within otherwise uncritical retail content, isolated moments of genuine sourcing caution are worth identifying and weighting appropriately, separate from the surrounding less careful material in the same source.

## LoreCodex Notes

Peruvian opal's primary value for SKILLd20 design is, somewhat unusually for this project, methodological rather than narrative: this entry is among the clearest demonstrations yet of the gap between a confidently and widely repeated claim and a claim's actual evidentiary support.

**The "by-product of a greater industry" theme is the strongest and most directly usable genuine design element in this entry.** A beautiful and culturally significant gem material discovered incidentally within a mining operation focused on an entirely different and more economically important resource (here, copper) is a clean, well-documented, and realistic template for a SKILLd20 material whose discovery story is economic and incidental rather than mythological -- a useful third variant alongside this project's "lost and rediscovered quarry" pattern (red porphyry, Mexican opal) and "boom-bust-boom" pattern (boulder opal): the material that was never specifically sought at all, but turned up while looking for something else entirely.

**If the design team wishes to use the Pachamama/earth-mother association in SKILLd20 content, this entry recommends doing so explicitly as a modern or recently-revived cultural association (consistent with the genuine, well-documented late-20th-century Pachamama veneration revival noted by Moonrise Crystals) rather than as a confidently-asserted pre-Columbian Inca tradition**, given this entry's inability to locate supporting primary sourcing for the latter framing despite a dedicated search effort. This is a more historically honest framing that does not require abandoning the genuinely evocative thematic pairing (an earth-toned stone associated with an earth goddess), only correctly dating and sourcing it.

**The "national stone of Peru" claim should not be repeated as verified fact in any LoreCodex content** without further direct confirmation. This entry's specific and dedicated search for a governmental or legal source found none, and the claim's near-universal but uncited repetition across commercial retail sources, combined with its conspicuous absence from the single most institutionally-careful source consulted, is recommended as a clean teaching example of how widely a claim can circulate without ever being traced to a verifiable origin -- comparable in mechanism, though distinct in subject matter, to the unverified "Mexican Diamond"/national-gemstone-of-Mexico claim already flagged with similar caution in this project's companion jelly opal entry.

**Recommended cross-reference and follow-up:** This entry's central open question -- whether a genuine, named, primary-sourced pre-Columbian Andean opal tradition exists -- mirrors the structurally similar and more successfully resolved questions already addressed for Mexican opal (via the Gubelin and Eckert citations) and deserves the same kind of dedicated, citation-seeking follow-up research, most plausibly through direct consultation of named Spanish colonial-era chroniclers of Andean religious practice, before any confident pre-Columbian opal-Pachamama claim is incorporated into LoreCodex content.
