## Plant Identity

**Scientific name:** *Mandragora officinarum* L. (also *M. autumnalis*, *M. vernalis*; taxonomy debated; treated as a single species by Linnaeus 1753, following Tournefort 1700)
**Common names:** Mandrake (English); Alraun / Alraune (German -- specifically the root as a talisman figure); Mandragora (classical Latin and Greek form; also modern Italian and some scholarly usage); Dudaim (Hebrew -- the biblical name; may derive from the root *dod*, love); Yabrouh / Yabruj (Arabic); Circe's root (classical poetic reference)
**Family:** Solanaceae (nightshade family -- also contains belladonna, henbane, datura, tobacco, potato, tomato)
**Native range:** Mediterranean basin -- southern Europe (Italy, Spain, Greece, the Balkans), North Africa, the Levant; grows as a weed in dry, disturbed soil and in wheat fields in the ancient Near East
**Appearance:** Large wrinkled leaves in a ground rosette; blue/purple flowers in autumn or spring (depending on variety); golden-yellow fruit the size of a small apple or plum, fragrant; the root is the defining feature -- long (often several feet), thick, frequently bifurcated into "legs," sometimes with lateral branches resembling arms, the head-end often covered with fibrous material resembling hair. The anthropomorphic root is the single most important fact about mandrake's entire mythological tradition.

**[PROTOCOL GAP -- New field needed: Active compounds and pharmacology]**

This field is essential for plants with documented physiological effects and is absent from the mineral protocol. Mandrake contains:
- **Tropane alkaloids** (primary class): hyoscyamine, scopolamine (hyoscine), atropine, mandragorine
- **Pharmacological effects:** Anticholinergic -- sedation, analgesia, hallucination (deliriant), dry mouth, dilated pupils, tachycardia, confusion; at higher doses, delirium, unconsciousness, and death
- **Modern pharmacological use:** Scopolamine is still used in anesthesia premedication and for motion sickness; atropine is used in ophthalmology and cardiac emergencies
- **Toxicity:** The whole plant is toxic; the root is most potent. Ancient dose descriptions (1 cyathus = approximately 45 ml of wine reduction) correspond to real anesthetic thresholds. Overdose causes death.
- **Real-world basis for myth:** Mandrake's tropane alkaloids cause delirium and hallucination before sedation. A person dosed with mandrake would experience a period of confusion, visual disturbance, and altered perception before losing consciousness. This pharmacological reality underlies every tradition about the plant's supernatural properties.

**[PROTOCOL GAP -- New field needed: Seasonal / cultivation notes]**

Mandrake fruits ripen in summer; the plant was typically found in wheat fields, which is why Reuben finds it "in the days of wheat harvest" (Genesis 30:14) -- a seasonally accurate detail that confirms the narrative's botanical authenticity. The plant is not cultivated easily; it was gathered wild. It was traded over long distances because its Mediterranean native range did not extend into Mesopotamia or Egypt, making it valuable and exotic wherever it appeared.

## Source Quality Summary

Mandrake has the richest mythological record of any plant in Western cultural history, with documented traditions in named primary texts spanning roughly 3,000 years, from the Hebrew Bible through the modern novel. The evidence base is primarily Tier 1-2 for the classical and biblical traditions and Tier 2-4 for the medieval European folklore. Almost every tradition about mandrake is documented in a named text; this is unusually strong sourcing for a plant.

The pharmacological record is equally strong: peer-reviewed analysis of mandrake's tropane alkaloids explains virtually every attributed property. The plant genuinely is an anesthetic, analgesic, fertility-associated plant (through its sedative and antispasmodic effects), and a hallucinogen. This means that unlike most gemstones in this project series, mandrake has a documented real-world basis not just for its color associations but for its primary attributed powers.

The most important sourcing precision point in this entry: **the screaming root tradition is a medieval European embellishment, not an ancient one.** The peer-reviewed *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine* paper (Dafni et al. 2021, named authors, named journal) explicitly documents that the screaming element only reached Europe in the 12th century and was not present in the first known record of the dog-uprooting ceremony (Josephus, 1st century CE, Judea). The dog-uprooting ritual is ancient; the lethal scream is medieval. This distinction matters for LoreCodex accuracy.

## Geographical Survey

### Middle East and North Africa

**Biblical / Hebrew** | Tier 1

Mandrake appears in two named biblical passages, constituting the oldest named documentary record for the plant in any tradition:

*Genesis 30:14-24* (c. 6th-5th century BCE composition, based on oral tradition): Reuben, son of Leah, finds mandrakes in the field during the wheat harvest. Rachel, who is barren, agrees to let Leah sleep with their shared husband Jacob in exchange for the mandrakes. Leah conceives. Rachel eventually conceives -- but Genesis 30:22 explicitly states "Then God remembered Rachel, and God listened to her and opened her womb." The narrative structure of Genesis 30 undermines the mandrake's attributed power: the text records the belief in mandrake's fertility properties while theologically negating it.

The University of Iowa Bible & Archaeology article (named peer-reviewed source) states that the exchange in Genesis 30:14-24 "constitutes an exchange of sex for fertility drugs" and that "the story is rooted in an ancient fertility myth in which mandrakes are understood to possess physical and chemical properties that enhance fertility." The Hebrew word *dudaim* may share a root with *dodim* ("love"), making the "love plant" etymology possible and supporting the pre-biblical antiquity of the fertility association.

*Song of Songs 7:13*: "The mandrakes give a fragrance, and over our doors are all choice fruits, both new and old, which I have laid up for you, O my beloved." This is a straightforward aphrodisiac/romantic context, consistent with the Genesis tradition. Named biblical text, Tier 1.

The mandrake appears in the Amarna Letters (mid-14th century BCE Egyptian diplomatic correspondence in Akkadian) and in Egyptian love songs as symbols of love and fertility -- the University of Iowa article cites these as context, though direct quotations of the Amarna mandrake references were not found in searched sources.

**Islamic / Arabic** | Tier 2

Mandrake is documented in the Islamic medical tradition through named major works. Ibn Sina (Avicenna, 980-1037) cites mandrake seed in his *Canon of Medicine* (al-Qanun fi al-Tibb) as a stupefacient for pain relief. The *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine* paper cites *Canon of Medicine*, Lib. II, Trsacii, Cap 365, where Ibn Sina also discusses the word *yabroukh* (Arabic for mandrake) as potentially referring to "a growing plant in human shape."

The Arabic and Farsi names for mandrake include terms meaning "dog killer" and "dog uprooter" (*Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine*, Dafni et al. 2021) -- names specifically referencing the ritual use of a dog in harvesting. The authors argue that this concentraton of dog-specific names in Arabic and Farsi suggests that the dog-uprooting tradition originated in the Middle East / Iran region rather than in Europe.

**Egypt** | Tier 2

Egyptian love songs mention mandrakes as symbols of love and fertility (cited in the University of Iowa article). The plants were not native to Egypt but were imported from the Levant. No specifically Egyptian mythological tradition was documented beyond the love/fertility association.

### Europe

**Greece and Rome** | Tier 1

This is the richest primary-source tradition for mandrake's medicinal and ritual properties. Multiple named ancient authors document the plant:

**Theophrastus** (*Historia Plantarum*, c. 372-287 BCE, named author, named text, Tier 1): The ScienceDirect paper on mandrake's history in medicine identifies *Historia Plantarum* (IX.9.1) as the earliest systematic botanical documentation of mandrake. Theophrastus describes the ritual harvesting requirement: "draw three circles around the root with a sword, and cut it facing west." He also records that to obtain a second piece, the harvester must dance around the plant while speaking as much lewd sexual talk as possible. These are the earliest documented harvesting rituals.

**Corpus Hippocraticum** (named text, pre-Theophrastus): Mandrake is documented in the Hippocratic corpus, confirming medical awareness from at least the 4th century BCE. The academia.edu paper on mandrake as anesthetic cites that Hippocrates, Demosthenes, Theophrastus, Celsus, and Apuleius all cited mandrake as a powerful narcotic drug.

**Dioscorides** (*De Materia Medica*, 1st century CE, named author, named text, Tier 1): The most important ancient medical authority for mandrake. Dioscorides describes the plant's uses as narcotic, analgesic, and abortifacient. He provides a dosage specification for surgical anesthesia: 1 cyathus (approximately 45 ml) of mandrake root boiled in wine until reduced to one third, administered before incisions or punctures. He claims a love potion can be concocted from mandrake. The Greek Dioscorides manuscript in the National Library of Vienna (*Vienna Dioscorides*, 512 CE) is the oldest surviving illuminated manuscript in the world and contains one of the most famous mandrake root illustrations, showing the plant with anthropomorphic features.

**Pliny the Elder** (*Natural History*, Book XXV, 77 CE, named author, named text, Tier 1): Documents male (white) and female (black) varieties of mandrake; describes it as potent and dangerous; repeats the 1 cyathus surgical dose. Pliny's account of the rituals may rely on Theophrastus.

**Circe's root:** The classical poetic tradition associated mandrake with Circe, the sorceress of Homer's *Odyssey*. The academia.edu paper is titled "From Circe's Root to Spongia Soporifera" -- citing the classical association between mandrake and the most famous literary drug-wielder in Western literature. Homer's Circe uses a pharmakeia ("drug" / "magical substance") to transform Odysseus's men; while the text does not specify the plant, ancient commentators and later tradition identified it with mandrake.

**Medieval Catholic Europe** | Tier 2-4

The medieval European mandrake tradition is documented in named works and named scholars:

**Hildegard von Bingen** (*Physica*, c. 1150-1160, named author, named text, Tier 4): Hildegard wrote that she believed mandrakes were made from the same clay God used to sculpt Adam. She describes the alraun (mandrake) as a plant with special power connected to human origin. The exact Physica passage on mandrake would need direct consultation for full precision; the source citing her (for-puck's-sake.org, Patheos) summarises this tradition.

The screaming root tradition is documented in medieval herbals from approximately the 12th century onward. The *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine* paper (named peer-reviewed source) states explicitly: "the screaming element only reached Europe from the twelfth century" (citing Van Arsdall et al.). The Old English Herbarium (c. 1000 CE) describes the mandrake as shining at night like a lantern -- an early European embellishment without a classical precedent.

**The *Grímnismál* / Grimms' *Deutsche Sagen*** : The Germanic *Alraun* tradition is documented in the Grimm brothers' *Deutsche Sagen* (1816, named authors, named text), which records the conditions required for a mandrake to grow at the gallows: a hanged man who was a hereditary thief, whose mother had either stolen or contemplated stealing while carrying him, who died a violent death. This is Tier 4 -- a named 19th-century compilation of documented oral tradition.

**Flavius Josephus** (*Jewish War*, Book VII, c. 75 CE, named author, named text, Tier 1): The first named document of the dog-uprooting ceremony. Josephus describes the plant *baara* (identified by most scholars as mandrake) that will kill anyone who tries to uproot it; to harvest it safely, a dog is tied to the root. Critically, there is **no mention of a scream** in Josephus -- this is confirmed by the *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine* peer-reviewed paper. The lethal scream was added by the medieval European tradition, not present in the oldest account.

**Jeanne d'Arc (Joan of Arc):** Documented at her 1431 trial: she was accused of carrying a mandrake root as evidence of witchcraft. The Wellcome Collection source documents this. This is a named historical event that confirms mandrake's association with witchcraft in 15th-century France and the danger of possessing the plant.

**The *Alraune* novel (Hanns Heinz Ewers, 1911):** The gallows-mandrake tradition entered literary culture through this named German novel, in which a scientist artificially inseminates a sex worker with the semen of a hanged murderer; the resulting child, Alraune, grows up without the capacity for love. This is Tier 4 -- named author, named text, 1911. It is the direct literary descendant of the medieval gallows tradition.

**Renaissance / Early Modern** | Tier 2-4

In the Renaissance, mandrake was a key ingredient in witches' flying ointments -- preparations applied to the skin (or broomsticks) to induce hallucinatory "flights." This tradition is documented in named early modern demonological texts. The tropane alkaloids in mandrake are transdermal-absorbable, making skin-application of mandrake-based ointments pharmacologically plausible. The "witches' sabbath" experience documented by accused witches (flying, attendance at night gatherings) is pharmacologically consistent with tropane alkaloid hallucination.

*Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet* (1597): "And shrieks like mandrakes' torn out of the earth, that living mortals, hearing them, run mad" (IV.3). Named author, named text, named scene. This is the most famous literary citation of the screaming mandrake and confirms the tradition's cultural saturation in Elizabethan England.

*Machiavelli, La Mandragola* (1518): A comedy named for and organized around mandrake's supposed fertility properties. Named author, named play, named date -- Tier 4.

**Norse / Scandinavian, Celtic, Slavic** | Tier 4-5

Mandrake is not native to northern Europe and does not appear in the Eddas or early Norse texts. Its appearance in Germanic traditions (Alraune, Galgenmännlein) is documented in medieval and early modern sources, not pre-Christian ones. No documented Celtic primary tradition found. Slavic mandrake traditions may exist but were not confirmed in searched sources.

### Asia

**India / Vedic / Ayurvedic** | Tier 5

Mandrake's range does not extend to India; it was not a native plant of the subcontinent. Some secondary sources suggest Ayurvedic awareness of mandrake through trade contacts, but no named Sanskrit text with a mandrake-specific entry was found. Tier 5.

**China, Japan, Korea, East Asia** | No documented tradition found.

**Central Asia / Persia / Iran** | Tier 2-3

The *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine* paper documents Persian and Arabic names for mandrake that specifically encode the dog-uprooting ritual ("dog killer," "dog uprooter" in Farsi), and argues based on this concentration of dog-specific names that the dog-uprooting tradition likely originated in Iran and adjacent countries. This positions Persia/Iran as the probable origin of one of mandrake's most famous traditions.

### The Americas, Sub-Saharan Africa, Pacific, Aboriginal Australian

**No documented tradition found** for any of these regions. Mandrake (*Mandragora officinarum*) is not native to any of these regions; no equivalent plant carries the same tradition. Note: "American mandrake" or mayapple (*Podophyllum peltatum*) is a distinct plant with its own medicinal properties but is not the same plant and does not carry the same tradition.

## Thematic Analysis

### Recurring Themes

**1. Fertility, Love, and Desire** (Dominant -- Tier 1 across Hebrew Bible, Song of Songs, Greek and Roman tradition)

The oldest documented tradition for mandrake is fertility and love -- encoded in the Hebrew *dudaim* (possibly from *dod*, love), attested in Genesis 30 and Song of Songs, referenced in the Amarna Letters and Egyptian love songs, and carried through Dioscorides (love potion) and Pliny to the Renaissance (Machiavelli's *La Mandragola*). This theme has a documented pharmacological basis: mandrake contains scopolamine, which at sub-anesthetic doses acts as a mild euphoric and disinhibitor. The belief in mandrake as a fertility and love drug encodes a real psychopharmacological observation.

**2. Anesthesia and Surgical Medicine** (Tier 1 -- classical medicine)

Mandrake's most precisely documented use across named primary sources is as a surgical anesthetic. Dioscorides' dosage specification (1 cyathus of boiled wine reduction before incisions), the anesthesia paper citing Homer, Hippocrates, Plato, Aristotle, Demosthenes, Theophrastus, Celsus, Apuleius, and Byzantine sources -- this is one of the most extensively primary-sourced medical uses of any plant in Western history. The medieval spongia soporifera (soporific sponge -- a sponge soaked in mandrake, henbane, opium, and other compounds and held under the patient's nose for surgical procedures) is documented in named medieval medical texts (Isidore of Seville, Theodoric of Cervia, Gilbertus Anglicus, William of Varignana, all named in the academia.edu paper). This tradition connects directly to the plant's chemistry: tropane alkaloids are genuine anesthetics.

**3. The Anthropomorphic Root and the Doctrine of Signatures** (Cross-cultural -- Tier 1-2)

The mandrake root's humanoid shape is the single most important physical fact generating the plant's mythology. The Doctrine of Signatures -- the ancient and medieval principle that a plant's physical form reveals its medicinal or magical properties -- explains how the humanoid root became a semi-demon, a fertility charm, a substitute for human sacrifice, and an alchemical homunculus. The *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine* paper identifies this as a documented ethnobotanical framework: "Mandrake is a classic exemplar of the 'Doctrine of Signatures.'"

The root's humanoid form generated three independent traditions:
- The fertility tradition: the plant resembles a person → it creates persons
- The harvesting danger tradition: the plant has a spirit inside it (because it looks like it does) → uprooting it harms that spirit → the spirit retaliates
- The alchemical tradition: the plant can be animated into a homunculus (the *Alraun* / *Galgenmännlein*)

**4. The Harvesting Ritual -- the Dog, the Sword, the Circles** (Tier 1-2 across Greek, Roman, Judean, Persian traditions)

The prescribed harvesting ritual for mandrake is one of the most extensively documented magical procedures in the history of Western occultism, with named primary sources going back to Theophrastus (3rd century BCE):
- Draw three circles with a sword (Theophrastus)
- Face west while cutting (Theophrastus)
- Dance and speak lewd sexual talk to obtain a second root (Theophrastus -- possibly related to fertility magic)
- Tie a dog to the root; the dog's lunge uproots the plant (Josephus, 1st century CE, first attestation of the dog)
- Plug ears; throw meat to make the dog run (standard elaboration in multiple traditions)

The screaming root that kills the hearer is a **medieval addition** (12th century onward in Europe), not present in Josephus or any earlier source. The dog-uprooting ceremony itself may originate in Iran/Persia based on the concentration of dog-specific Persian and Arabic folk names for the plant (Dafni et al. 2021).

**5. The Gallows, the Liminal, and the Alraune** (Medieval Germanic -- Tier 2-4)

The Germanic tradition that mandrake grows beneath gallows where executed criminals spilled their bodily fluids is the most overtly dark tradition in this project and one of the most extensively documented in named folklore texts. The Grimm brothers' *Deutsche Sagen* records the gallows requirements. The *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine* paper documents 14 European folk names referencing gallows, concentrated in Germany (7) and the Netherlands (4). This tradition positions mandrake at the intersection of death and fertility (corpse as seed), of criminal liminality and magical potency, and of the human and the vegetable. The gallows tradition is confirmed as "late-Medieval European origin" by the Dafni et al. paper -- it does not appear in classical, biblical, or Islamic tradition.

Hildegard von Bingen's statement that mandrake was made from the same clay as Adam provides a theologically positive valence to the same root-anthropomorphism: if the root looks like a person, it is because it was made of the same material as the first person.

### Theme Coherence Assessment

The fertility/love and anesthesia traditions have the clearest real-world basis -- the plant genuinely affects fertility-adjacent physiology (scopolamine as antispasmodic and disinhibitor) and genuinely anesthetizes. These traditions encode accurate observations.

The anthropomorphic root is the explanatory engine for virtually everything else: it explains why the plant has a spirit, why it screams, why it is used in place of human sacrifice, why it grows under gallows, and why it was used as a fertility charm. The root's shape is the single physical property most responsible for the plant's entire mythological tradition, more than its chemistry.

The screaming root is the clearest example in this project of a tradition that grew in the re-telling. Named primary sources trace the evolution: Theophrastus (ritual but no scream), Josephus (dog-uprooting but no scream), medieval European herbals from the 12th century (the scream that kills), Shakespeare (literary saturation of the lethal scream). The tradition became more elaborate, more dramatic, and more specific as it aged -- the opposite of most folk traditions, which typically simplify.

**Dominant overarching theme:** Mandrake is the plant of the threshold between human and vegetable, medicine and poison, life and death, the natural and the supernatural. Every tradition that has engaged with it has done so through the lens of the plant's uncanny resemblance to the human body -- a resemblance that pharmacologically accurate hallucinogens made genuinely strange in anyone who encountered the plant in a medicinal context.

**Genuine outlier:**

The surgical anesthesia tradition is almost entirely absent from the folk mythology. Dioscorides, Galen, and the medieval soporific sponge tradition are the medicinal record; the folk tradition (screaming root, gallows origin, fertility charm) is almost entirely separate from the medical tradition even though both clearly reference the same plant. This is one of the sharpest folk/medical tradition divisions in the project.

## Fictional Tradition

**Shakespeare, *Romeo and Juliet* (1597):** "And shrieks like mandrakes' torn out of the earth, that living mortals, hearing them, run mad" (IV.3). Named author, named play, named act/scene. The most famous literary citation of the screaming mandrake, documenting the tradition's cultural saturation in Elizabethan England.

**Machiavelli, *La Mandragola* (1518):** A satirical comedy built around a scheme using mandrake's supposed fertility properties to manipulate a young wife's husband. Named author, named play, named date. Still performed.

**Hanns Heinz Ewers, *Alraune* (1911):** Named author, named novel. The gallows tradition's literary heir: a child created from the semen of a hanged murderer.

**J.K. Rowling, *Harry Potter and the Chamber of Secrets* (1998):** Named author, named book. Mandrakes whose screams can revive Petrified victims and stun those who hear them during transplanting. **Dungeons & Dragons:** Mandrake root appears as a spell component and alchemical ingredient in D&D and Pathfinder, primarily associated with sleep, illusion, and charm effects. No named D&D lore specific to mandrake beyond spell component use found.

## LoreCodex Notes

Mandrake is the richest design source in the project for a plant that directly crosses the plant/magic interface, and it requires almost no invention -- every tradition documented above is primary-sourced and mechanically coherent.

**The pharmacological hook is the most valuable and most unusual.** Mandrake is the one plant in the likely LoreCodex set where the attributed magical properties are pharmacologically accurate. A "sleep potion" in SKILLd20 made from mandrake root is not metaphorical -- it is documented in Dioscorides and Pliny with specific dosages that correspond to real anesthetic thresholds. A spell component system that grounds magical effects in documented physical properties has exceptional historical support here.

**The ritual harvesting tradition** -- three circles with a sword, facing west, lewd speech for a second root, a dog to uproot -- is one of the most fully documented magical procedures in Western history. Translated directly into a SKILLd20 material component ritual, it would be a complete and historically grounded procedure requiring specific actions, timing, and tools to safely obtain a dangerous ingredient.

**The screaming root** is mechanically interesting precisely because its sourcing is documented: the scream is medieval, not ancient. A LoreCodex that notes this distinction can offer a world in which some in-fiction scholarly traditions accept the scream (following medieval sources) and others reject it (following Josephus), creating an internal debate that mirrors the actual historical record.

**The Doctrine of Signatures** is the key design principle for plants generally. If mandrake looks like a human body and is used for human-body effects (fertility, anesthesia, pain), the same principle can be applied to other plants in the LoreCodex: a plant that looks like a heart is used for heart conditions; a plant whose root looks like a bone strengthens bones; etc. This is documented ancient and medieval medical theory, not invention.

**The gallows/Alraune tradition** -- mandrake grown from the semen of executed criminals, animated as a homunculus, kept in silk and fed milk, revealing hidden treasure -- is a complete magical item description requiring no invention and with named sources going back to the Grimm brothers' documented oral tradition.
